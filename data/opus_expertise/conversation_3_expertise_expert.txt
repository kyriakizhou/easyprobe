HUMAN: I'm researching mean reversion strategies in equity markets. Traditional technical indicators like Bollinger bands seem over-arbitraged. What alternative signals have you seen show promise in recent years?

A: Classic technical signals are indeed crowded and often net-negative after transaction costs. More defensible approaches combine price action with microstructure data or cross-sectional information. With your order flow data, signals like bid-ask bounce, volume-weighted reversion, and informed trader detection tend to have more persistence. What's your typical holding period and universe?

H: Holding period is 1-5 days, universe is US mid-caps, roughly 500 names. We have high-frequency order book data but haven't exploited it much for the longer horizon strategy.

A: Interesting setup. For 1-5 day horizons, order book data can still add value, primarily as a filter rather than primary signal. Consider: using order flow imbalance to identify whether a price move is likely noise or informed trading, price impact asymmetry (how prices respond differently to buys vs sells can indicate directional conviction), and inventory models that track dealer positioning.

H: We've tested residualized returns against Fama-French factors but predictability is weak. Are there better factor models for this?

A: Fama-French is quite coarse for short-horizon trading. Alternatives: PCA-based statistical factors extracted from your universe (more adaptive to current market structure), higher-frequency sector and industry factors, and momentum/reversal factors calibrated to your holding period. The key is removing explainable variance to isolate idiosyncratic moves that might revert. Also consider conditional models - mean reversion behaves differently in trending vs mean-reverting regimes.

H: What's your view on alternative data for this strategy? We've looked at sentiment but found it noisy.

A: Sentiment suffers from crowding - everyone uses the same social media feeds. More differentiated sources: supply chain disruption signals, credit card transaction patterns, web traffic for consumer-facing companies, patent filing analysis. The alpha is in proprietary data or novel combinations. Alternative data often works better as a filter (avoid trading against strong signal) than as a primary driver.

H: How should we think about capacity estimation? We want to size our capital allocation appropriately.

A: Capacity depends on: universe liquidity (mid-caps have meaningful constraints), position sizes relative to ADV (staying under 1-2% of ADV for multi-day holds), expected turnover, and market impact model accuracy. For mid-caps with 1-5 day horizons, estimate impact using square-root models or Kyle's lambda calibrated from your order book data. Then simulate realistic execution on historical data. Most quant strategies disappoint on capacity estimates - be conservative.

H: Our backtests assume 10bps average slippage but live trading is worse. How do we improve execution?

A: 10bps is optimistic for mid-caps at size. Improvements: time-slice orders to reduce market impact, use IS (implementation shortfall) algorithms rather than VWAP for better price optimization, trade during higher liquidity periods (avoid open/close where spreads widen), and for entries consider limit orders that provide liquidity. Also measure realized slippage meticulously and feed back into backtest calibration.

H: This is valuable perspective. I'll focus on the order flow signals and more realistic execution modeling.

A: Good priorities. The edge in liquid equities is increasingly in execution and risk management rather than signal generation. Institutional-quality execution infrastructure often matters more than marginal signal improvements.
