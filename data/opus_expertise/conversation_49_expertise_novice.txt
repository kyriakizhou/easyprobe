HUMAN: How does GPS know where I am? Does it use cell towers?

A: Common misconception! GPS primarily uses satellites, not cell towers. There are about 30 GPS satellites orbiting Earth. Your phone receives signals from multiple satellites and calculates your position based on how long each signal took to arrive. Cell towers can assist, but satellites do the main work.

H: Wait, my phone talks to satellites?

A: It receives signals from them, yes! The satellites constantly broadcast their position and the exact time. Your phone picks up these signals from at least four satellites simultaneously. By comparing the tiny differences in arrival times, it calculates exactly where you must be.

H: How can it be so precise?

A: Math and timing! Those satellites have incredibly accurate atomic clocks. Even nanosecond differences in signal arrival times translate to position information. With signals from multiple satellites, your phone can triangulate your location to within a few meters. More satellites mean better accuracy.

H: Does GPS use my data plan?

A: The GPS signal itself is free - it comes directly from satellites. However, map apps use data to download map images and calculate routes. If you download offline maps, you can navigate without using data. The GPS positioning works even in airplane mode or with no cell service.

H: Why doesn't GPS work well indoors?

A: The satellite signals are relatively weak and struggle to penetrate buildings. Walls, roofs, and especially metal block or distort the signals. Indoors, your phone might use WiFi networks or cell towers to estimate your location instead, which is less precise than true GPS.

H: What about in tunnels or underground?

A: Same problem - no satellite signals can reach. Your phone might use its last known position, estimate based on your direction and speed, or switch to other methods if available. Some tunnels have beacon systems that help, but generally GPS won't work underground.

H: Can someone track me through GPS?

A: Your phone's GPS receiver only receives signals - it doesn't broadcast your location. However, apps on your phone can read your GPS location and send it elsewhere. This is why apps ask permission to access your location. You control which apps can know where you are through your phone's settings.

H: What did people do before GPS?

A: Paper maps and asking for directions! Navigation required planning routes in advance and following road signs. Getting lost was common. GPS was originally a military system that became available to civilians in the 1980s. Smartphones with GPS only became common around 2007. It's transformed how we navigate.

H: I never realized satellites were involved. That's pretty amazing!

A: It really is! We casually use a system of satellites orbiting at 12,500 miles altitude to find the nearest coffee shop. When you think about the technology involved, it's remarkable that it works so well and we take it for granted.
