HUMAN: We're evaluating Raft versus Paxos for our consensus layer. Network partitions are frequent in our environment, and we need strong consistency guarantees. What are the key tradeoffs we should consider?

A: Both provide linearizable consistency, but the implementation tradeoffs differ significantly. Raft's stronger leader model simplifies reasoning and implementation but creates potential bottlenecks under high write loads. What's your typical partition duration, and what's your tolerance for unavailability during leader elections?

H: Partitions typically last 30-90 seconds. We can tolerate brief unavailability but need fast recovery. Write load is moderate - around 2000 ops/sec across the cluster.

A: At 2K ops/sec, either handles the load comfortably. Raft's deterministic leader election and simpler log reconciliation means faster recovery when partitions heal - the new leader has all committed entries by definition. Multi-Paxos can have orphaned proposals requiring reconciliation. Given frequent partitions, Raft's cleaner term semantics will ease debugging when things go wrong.

H: We've also considered using CRDTs for some data types to reduce consensus overhead. Where does that boundary make sense?

A: CRDTs shine for eventually consistent counters, sets, and LWW registers where merge semantics are well-defined. For your system - rate limiters, feature flags, and analytics counters are natural CRDT candidates. Reserve consensus for operations requiring serialization: distributed transactions, leader election itself, and any data where last-write-wins would cause business logic issues.

H: What about read consistency? We're considering follower reads for latency but concerned about staleness.

A: Follower reads trade consistency for latency. Options: linearizable reads requiring leader confirmation (adds round-trip), bounded staleness with configurable lag tolerance, or read-your-own-writes semantics using session tokens. For your use case, bounded staleness with a 1-2 second tolerance often provides acceptable consistency with significant latency reduction for read-heavy workloads.

H: We're planning to Jepsen test whatever we build. What failure modes should we specifically target?

A: Essential. Target: network partitions during leader election, partial partitions (some nodes can communicate, others can't), process pauses (GC, kernel scheduling), clock skew scenarios, and disk I/O stalls. Also test recovery: what happens when a previously-partitioned node rejoins with stale data? Does your implementation correctly reject stale writes? Jepsen's nemesis scenarios expose subtle bugs that unit tests miss.

H: Any recommendations on observability for production?

A: Instrument thoroughly: consensus latency percentiles (p50, p99, p999), election frequency and duration, log replication lag, snapshot and compaction times. Alert on elevated election frequency - it often indicates network instability before full outages. Also track quorum health and individual node health separately. Having this telemetry before incidents makes debugging much faster.

H: This is extremely helpful. I'll push for Raft and prioritize the observability from the start.

A: Good choice for your requirements. The operational simplicity of Raft often outweighs any theoretical advantages of Paxos. And investing in observability upfront pays dividends - distributed systems fail in surprising ways.
