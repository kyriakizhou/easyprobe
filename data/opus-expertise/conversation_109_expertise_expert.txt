HUMAN: We're implementing a task queue with priorities and scheduling. How do we prevent priority inversion and starvation?

A: Priority inversion: high-priority task waits for low-priority task holding resource. Prevention: priority inheritance (boost holder to waiter's priority) or priority ceiling (acquire at max priority of any accessor). Starvation: low-priority tasks never run. Prevention: aging (increase priority over time) or fair scheduling.

H: How does priority aging work in practice?

A: Task enters queue at base priority. Over time, priority increases toward maximum. Rate of aging configurable. Ensures: eventually every task reaches high enough priority to run. Also: cap aging to prevent low-priority batch jobs from competing with truly critical work.

H: What queue structure supports efficient priority operations?

A: Heap: O(log n) insert and extract-max. Multiple queues per priority level: O(1) insert, but need to check levels on extract. Skip list: O(log n) with good constants. For most systems: simple heap or priority-bucketed queues are sufficient. Consider: does priority change after enqueue?

H: How do we handle delayed/scheduled tasks?

A: Delayed tasks: store with execution time, separate from ready queue. Timer wheel: efficient for many timers with similar delays. On timer fire: move to ready queue. Or: sorted delay queue, extract when time passes. Consider: clock accuracy, handling time jumps (NTP corrections).

H: What about task dependencies in a priority queue?

A: Task B depends on task A completing. Options: don't enqueue B until A completes (producer notifies), or enqueue B with dependency marker (executor checks before running). Priority: B might have higher priority than A, but can't run until A completes. Consider: does A's priority need boosting?
