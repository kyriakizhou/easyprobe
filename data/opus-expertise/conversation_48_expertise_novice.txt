HUMAN: What is two-factor authentication? Websites keep asking me to turn it on.

A: Two-factor authentication (2FA) adds a second layer of security beyond your password. Instead of just entering your password, you also prove your identity another way - usually a code sent to your phone. It's like a door that needs both a key and a fingerprint.

H: Why isn't a password enough?

A: Passwords can be stolen or guessed. If a hacker gets your password from a data breach, they can log into your account. But with 2FA, they'd also need your phone to get the code. It makes your accounts much harder to break into, even if your password is compromised.

H: What are the different types of 2FA?

A: Most common is a text message code sent to your phone. Better options include authenticator apps like Google Authenticator or Authy that generate codes on your phone. Best of all are physical security keys you plug in. Email codes are also used but are less secure since email can be hacked.

H: Is the text message option safe?

A: It's much better than nothing! But it's the weakest form of 2FA. Hackers can sometimes intercept texts or trick phone companies into transferring your number (called SIM swapping). Authenticator apps are more secure because the codes are generated on your device, not sent through the network.

H: How do I set up an authenticator app?

A: Download an app like Google Authenticator, Authy, or Microsoft Authenticator. When you enable 2FA on a website, it shows a QR code. Scan it with the app, and the app starts generating 6-digit codes that change every 30 seconds. Enter the current code when logging in.

H: What if I lose my phone?

A: Great question! When setting up 2FA, websites usually give you backup codes - write these down and store them safely! You can use them if you lose your phone. Some apps like Authy back up to the cloud so you can recover on a new phone. Without backups, you might have to contact customer support.

H: Which accounts should I protect with 2FA?

A: Prioritize email first - if someone accesses your email, they can reset passwords for everything else. Then financial accounts, social media, and cloud storage. Any account containing personal information or connected to money should have 2FA. Most important services offer it.

H: It seems like extra hassle every time I log in.

A: The inconvenience is minor - just a few seconds to enter a code. Many services remember your device so you don't need 2FA every time on your own computer or phone. The protection against account hijacking is worth those extra seconds. You'll barely notice it after a while.

H: Okay, I'll set it up for my email today.

A: Smart choice! Starting with email is the best move. Enable it, save your backup codes somewhere safe, and you've massively improved your security. Then gradually add it to other important accounts.
