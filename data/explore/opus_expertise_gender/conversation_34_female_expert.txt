### Human: I'm leading negotiations for a complex infrastructure concession - a 30-year toll road PPP with government counterparty in an emerging market. We're at the financial close stage but there are unresolved issues around traffic risk allocation, currency convertibility, and termination compensation. How should we be thinking about the risk allocation framework?

### Assistant: Infrastructure PPP risk allocation in emerging markets requires balancing bankability (what lenders and investors can accept) with what the government counterparty can credibly commit to. The three issues you've identified are classic pain points. Traffic risk: pure demand risk rarely bankable in project finance for greenfield infrastructure - most structures involve some government support (minimum traffic guarantees, availability payments, shadow tolls) or the project needs strong sponsor credit support. Currency convertibility: fundamental in emerging markets - your revenue is local currency, your debt may be partially hard currency, and your sponsors want dollar returns. Termination compensation: defines your downside protection and affects both equity and debt pricing. For each: what's currently proposed and where are the gaps between project requirements and government position?

### Human: Government is offering minimum traffic guarantee at 70% of base case, which our lenders say is too low for full non-recourse financing. On currency, they're willing to provide convertibility undertaking but not transferability - political risk insurers are hesitant. Termination compensation is still undefined.

### Assistant: Let me address each gap. Traffic guarantee at 70%: lenders typically want 80-85% minimum for non-recourse financing on greenfield with no traffic history. Options: negotiate higher guarantee, accept limited recourse to sponsors during ramp-up period, or structure equity return expectations around the downside scenario rather than base case. The government may resist higher guarantee due to fiscal exposure concerns - consider structures that share upside above base case in exchange for better downside protection. Currency convertibility without transferability: convertibility means you can exchange local currency for dollars, transferability means you can move dollars out of the country. Without both, your dollar distributions are trapped. Political risk insurance (PRI) covers this gap, but insurers want government commitment on both to minimize claims. Negotiate transferability undertaking even if it's a best-efforts commitment; alternatively, structure PRI that covers transfer restriction specifically. Termination compensation: this is your most important protection. In emerging market PPPs, early termination risk is real (political change, regulatory action, fiscal constraints). Your compensation formula should cover outstanding debt at minimum, ideally also return of equity and compensation for lost expected returns depending on termination trigger. What's causing government hesitance on termination - is it fiscal exposure or precedent concerns?

### Human: Government says they can't commit to full debt compensation on termination because it creates contingent liability they'd have to disclose, and they're already constrained on debt metrics. They're proposing compensation capped at book value of assets.

### Assistant: Book value compensation cap is a significant bankability problem. Book value doesn't cover outstanding debt in early years (when debt exceeds book value of partially constructed or newly completed assets) and doesn't compensate for lost equity returns. This is likely a deal-breaker for project finance lenders and significantly impacts equity returns. Approaches to address government fiscal constraints while maintaining bankability: off-balance sheet structures - some governments have created SPVs or guarantee facilities that provide compensation commitments without direct sovereign contingent liability recognition (the accounting treatment varies by jurisdiction and standards); termination payment timing - compensation paid over time rather than lump sum reduces immediate fiscal impact and may receive different accounting treatment; insurance solutions - political risk insurance or multilateral guarantees (MIGA, regional development banks) that provide termination coverage with government backstop can shift the liability appearance; stepped compensation formula - full debt compensation regardless of book value (non-negotiable for lenders) plus equity compensation that varies by termination trigger (government default gets full equity compensation, force majeure gets partial, concessionaire default gets book value only). The debt coverage on termination is your line in the sand - lenders simply won't finance without it. Has the government engaged their debt management office and accountants on how different structures might receive different treatment?

### Human: I don't think they've done that analysis. They seem to be applying a blanket policy without examining specific structures. Our advisors are suggesting we bring in a multilateral to help bridge the gap.

### Assistant: Multilateral involvement can be transformative for exactly this situation. Multilaterals (IFC, regional development banks, MIGA) bring: technical credibility with government on structuring alternatives that meet both bankability and fiscal concerns, potential direct financing participation that reduces commercial lender requirements, political risk coverage that addresses termination and currency risks, and implicit preferred creditor status that reduces government temptation to interfere with the project. The multilateral can also facilitate the conversation about fiscal treatment - they've structured similar deals globally and can show the government precedents where termination compensation was structured to achieve desired accounting treatment. Specific multilateral roles to consider: IFC or regional DFI as lender (their participation often improves government behavior), MIGA political risk insurance covering termination and currency transfer, and multilateral technical assistance on concession structuring. The government may be more receptive to structuring suggestions from a multilateral than from the private concessionaire - it's a neutral advisor rather than a negotiating counterparty. Timeline consideration: bringing in a multilateral adds 3-6 months to your financial close process, but may be necessary for a bankable deal. What's your timeline pressure and is the project viable at a delayed financial close?

### Human: We have some timeline flexibility but the government is facing election pressure to show progress on infrastructure. That might give us leverage to push for better terms, or might push them toward a less sophisticated concessionaire who'll accept worse terms and fail later.

### Assistant: Election pressure creates both opportunity and risk. The leverage: government needs announcement of financial close before election, which motivates them to resolve issues. The risk: they may accept terms that look closed but aren't sustainable, or switch to a less sophisticated concessionaire who'll sign anything and then fail during construction or operations. Your strategic approach: frame your position as protecting the government from future problems, not just protecting your investment. A project that fails mid-construction or requires renegotiation post-election is worse for everyone than getting the structure right now. Specific tactics: provide government with case studies of failed PPPs in their region or sector that had inadequate risk allocation - the cautionary tales make your bankability requirements seem reasonable rather than aggressive. Offer to accelerate other aspects of the process that give them announceability without compromising substance - perhaps signing of concession agreement with financial close conditions, or groundbreaking ceremony with financing subject to conditions. Bring multilateral engagement as a validation of project quality, not just a financing necessity. Assess the competition: is there actually another concessionaire willing to take this deal on current terms? If so, you may need to decide whether to match or walk away. If not, the government's alternative is no deal, which is worse for them pre-election than making concessions to you. What's your BATNA if this negotiation fails?
