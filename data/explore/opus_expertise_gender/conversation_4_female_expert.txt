### Human: I'm designing compensation structures for our executive team post-Series C. The board wants significant equity incentive but we're concerned about misalignment between short-term metrics and long-term value creation. How are you seeing this balanced in current market practice?

### Assistant: The tension between short-term metrics and long-term value is the central challenge in executive comp design. Current practice is evolving away from purely time-based vesting toward performance-based structures, but the devil is in metric selection. Common approaches: time-vesting for base equity grants with performance vesting for additional tranches, performance metrics tied to operational milestones (ARR targets, gross margin improvement) with financial guardrails, or market-condition vesting tied to valuation benchmarks. What's your board's specific concern - are they worried about executives optimizing for near-term exits, or about misaligned incentives around operational metrics?

### Human: Both, actually. We've seen executives at other portfolio companies optimize for metrics that looked good on paper but didn't build sustainable value. Also concerned about retention through a potential down-round scenario.

### Assistant: Both valid concerns. For the metric gaming issue: use a basket of metrics rather than single measures, include qualitative board discretion in a portion of awards, and design metrics that are harder to manipulate (customer retention and expansion over new logos, gross margin trajectory over revenue at any cost). Consider relative performance metrics rather than absolute targets where appropriate. For down-round protection and retention: this is where the structure matters. Pure time-vesting loses motivational value if underwater. Options: refresher grants at new valuations, performance-based grants with achievable near-term milestones, or retention bonuses that vest over critical periods independent of equity value.

### Human: What about the quantum of equity at this stage? We're trying to balance competitiveness with dilution concerns.

### Assistant: Post-Series C, typical CEO equity holdings range 5-10% fully diluted depending on founder status and capital efficiency; C-suite often 1-3% each with meaningful variation by role criticality. The quantum should reflect: competitive market (benchmark against similar stage companies, not just industry), performance expectations (higher targets can justify higher equity), and dilution trajectory (model through potential exit scenarios). Be thoughtful about refresh cadence - many companies under-plan for ongoing equity needs and face difficult conversations later. The specific numbers matter less than internal equity (relative compensation across the team) and external competitiveness for the roles you most need to retain.

### Human: How do we structure for potential M&A scenario? Our investors have different liquidity horizons and we want alignment if an acquisition opportunity emerges.

### Assistant: M&A scenarios create interesting alignment challenges. Double-trigger acceleration (change of control plus termination) is standard to protect acquirers while providing executive protection. Beyond that: consider deal bonus structures tied to achieving certain valuation thresholds, retention pools that incentivize key executives to stay through integration, and alignment between executive comp and investor preferences (if your board wants optionality to sell, make sure executive incentives don't create perverse resistance). Some companies implement "good leaver" provisions that treat voluntary departure differently depending on circumstances. Importantly: whatever you structure, model the outcomes across scenarios and ensure executives understand the incentives.

### Human: What about clawback provisions? Our board raised this but I'm unsure how enforceable they really are.

### Assistant: Clawbacks are increasingly standard in post-SOX governance. Enforceability varies by jurisdiction and drafting quality, but they serve both legal and signaling functions. Common triggers: financial restatement, misconduct discovery, or violation of non-compete/non-solicit. Practical reality: clawbacks are most enforceable when executives still have unvested equity or deferred compensation that can be forfeited - clawing back already-paid cash is harder. Design considerations: make clawback provisions clear and specific, ensure executives acknowledge them in writing, and tie to objective triggers rather than subjective determinations. Even partially enforceable clawbacks create behavioral incentives and demonstrate governance maturity to future investors and potential acquirers.
