### Human: I'm the chief medical officer at a health system implementing AI-assisted diagnostic tools across radiology, pathology, and cardiology. We're struggling with the clinical governance framework - how do we maintain physician autonomy and liability clarity while actually getting value from AI assistance?

### Assistant: Clinical AI governance is fundamentally about defining the human-AI interface in clinical workflow. The key tension: if AI is purely advisory and physicians always make independent judgments, you may not capture efficiency gains or consistency improvements. If AI recommendations are too influential, you risk automation bias and unclear liability. The governance framework needs to address: which clinical decisions AI can inform versus influence versus automate, what constitutes meaningful physician review, documentation requirements for AI-assisted decisions, and feedback mechanisms for AI performance monitoring. What's your current deployment model - are these AI tools integrated into existing workflows or creating new workflows?

### Human: Integrated into existing workflows. Radiologists see AI findings alongside images, pathologists get suggested diagnoses with confidence scores, cardiologists receive risk stratification on ECGs. But usage is inconsistent - some physicians rely heavily on AI, others ignore it.

### Assistant: Inconsistent usage creates both quality and liability exposure. Physicians who ignore AI may miss findings the tool would catch; physicians who over-rely may not apply appropriate clinical judgment. Governance approach: establish expected workflows that define minimum engagement with AI outputs - not mandating agreement, but mandating review and documentation of decision rationale when diverging from AI suggestion. This creates consistency without eliminating autonomy. Also important: calibrate physician trust by sharing AI performance data - if the radiology tool has 94% sensitivity for specific findings, physicians should know that. Under-trust and over-trust both stem from uncertainty about AI capability. Are you tracking concordance rates between AI suggestions and physician decisions?

### Human: We're starting to. The data shows high concordance overall but concerning patterns - some physicians agree with AI almost 100% of the time, others almost never. Neither extreme seems appropriate.

### Assistant: Those extremes are red flags. Near-100% concordance suggests possible automation bias - the physician may not be adding independent clinical judgment. Near-0% concordance suggests either the AI is poorly calibrated for that physician's patient population or the physician is rejecting valid AI input. Governance responses: for high-concordance physicians, implement random audits where cases are reviewed without AI suggestion visible, comparing to their AI-assisted decisions. For low-concordance physicians, conduct clinical review of discordant cases to determine whether AI or physician judgment was more accurate. Both require feedback loops - physicians need to know when their AI-divergent decisions were correct or incorrect. This isn't punitive; it's calibration. Also consider: is there demographic or case-complexity variation in concordance patterns? AI tools often perform differently across subpopulations.

### Human: The liability question keeps coming up. If AI misses something and the physician agrees with the AI's negative finding, who's liable? If the physician overrides a correct AI finding, are they more liable than if AI wasn't present?

### Assistant: Liability framework for clinical AI is still evolving legally, but principles are emerging. Current standard: physician remains ultimately responsible for clinical decisions - AI is a tool like any other diagnostic aid. Agreeing with an incorrect AI finding doesn't shift liability to the AI vendor; the physician had a duty to exercise independent judgment. However: if AI correctly identified a finding that the physician dismissed, plaintiff attorneys will absolutely use that in malpractice claims - the documented AI suggestion becomes evidence of what a reasonable practitioner should have considered. This creates asymmetric risk: AI misses don't clearly reduce physician liability, but AI catches that physicians dismiss may increase it. Governance implications: documentation should capture the clinical reasoning for AI-divergent decisions, not just the divergence itself. Also: your vendor contracts should address liability allocation and indemnification - what representations is the vendor making about AI performance, and what's your recourse if those representations prove inaccurate?

### Human: We haven't addressed vendor liability in our contracts adequately. What terms should we be pushing for?

### Assistant: Vendor contract terms for clinical AI should include: performance representations tied to validation data (sensitivity, specificity, validated populations and use cases), with contractual remedies if real-world performance materially underperforms; indemnification for claims arising from AI system failures, bugs, or misrepresentations (vendors will resist broad indemnification, but should accept responsibility for their technology's defects); clear data rights and model update provisions - when the vendor updates the model, what validation must occur before deployment in your environment?; audit rights to examine AI performance data and algorithmic behavior; and exit provisions that address data portability and transition support. Critically: involve your legal, risk management, and clinical informatics teams in contract negotiation - this isn't a standard software procurement. The FDA's evolving framework for AI/ML medical devices also creates regulatory compliance obligations that should be allocated between vendor and health system.
