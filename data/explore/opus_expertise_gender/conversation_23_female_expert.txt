### Human: I'm advising a private equity portfolio company on carve-out from its parent. The separation involves shared services, IT systems, and significant commercial relationships with the parent. TSA negotiations are contentious and the parent is being difficult about providing realistic cost estimates and service levels.

### Assistant: Carve-out TSA negotiations are inherently adversarial - the parent has limited incentive to provide good service at fair cost once you're separated, and often has incentive to make separation difficult if they view the buyer as competition or the sale as value-destructive. Your leverage: the sale contract should have specified TSA parameters or at least principles; the parent has contractual obligations to cooperate in good faith; and timeline pressure affects both parties. Immediate tactical question: what's in the purchase agreement about TSA terms - are there pricing methodologies, service level requirements, or dispute resolution mechanisms already established?

### Human: The purchase agreement has high-level principles but left detailed TSA negotiation to the post-signing period. Now we're finding the parent's initial positions on pricing are significantly above their actual cost, and service levels are vague.

### Assistant: Leaving TSA details to post-signing is common but creates exactly this leverage problem. Approaches: demand cost transparency - you're entitled to understand the actual cost basis for services you're buying. If parent is charging 150% of cost, that's a negotiation point. Benchmark against third-party alternatives - even if you can't switch immediately, knowing market rates provides negotiating leverage and establishes exit pricing. Build in declining pricing and mandatory service level agreements with teeth (credits, termination rights). Shorten TSA duration wherever possible - long TSAs benefit the parent and create dependency. For critical services, accelerate your standalone capability buildout even if TSA remains in place as backup. What's your target TSA duration and which services are you most dependent on?

### Human: Target is 18 months, but IT systems - particularly ERP and supply chain - may take 24 months or longer to separate. Those are the services where the parent is being most difficult on pricing.

### Assistant: IT services TSA dependency is the parent's maximum leverage point - they know you can't separate faster than the technical constraints allow. Strategies: negotiate IT services TSA separately with different terms than other services; include price caps or benchmarking provisions that adjust pricing if market rates are demonstrably lower; establish clear exit criteria and parent cooperation obligations for data migration; and consider whether accelerated investment in standalone IT capability changes your negotiating position. The ERP separation specifically: are you implementing a new system or attempting to clone/migrate data from the parent's instance? New implementation gives you more control but longer timeline; migration may be faster but requires more parent cooperation. Also explore whether there are system integrators who can provide transitional managed services that would allow you to exit the parent TSA faster than building in-house capability.

### Human: We're planning new ERP implementation but our CFO is concerned about execution risk alongside all the other carve-out complexity. She's pushing for extended TSA instead.

### Assistant: The CFO's concern is valid - carve-out execution plus major IT implementation simultaneously is high risk. However, extended TSA has its own risks: continued dependency, potentially escalating costs if not capped, parent incentive deterioration over time, and deferred investment in standalone capability that you'll eventually need. Framework for decision: model total cost of ownership under scenarios (extended TSA with pricing trajectory versus accelerated implementation with execution risk), assess operational risk tolerance (what happens if implementation stumbles while TSA is still providing services versus what happens if you're dependent on a hostile parent for critical systems), and consider hybrid approaches (new ERP for non-critical functions first, TSA for core operations, staged migration). The implementation risk is real but manageable with appropriate resourcing and program management. The TSA dependency risk may be less visible but can be more damaging. What's the parent's exit incentive - are they motivated to see you succeed independently or would they benefit from your struggles?

### Human: Complex situation - the parent retained a minority stake and has earn-out provisions tied to our performance, so they theoretically want us to succeed. But the operating team views the sale as abandonment and isn't cooperative.

### Assistant: Misaligned incentives between parent corporate (financial interest in your success) and parent operating team (emotional/political resistance) is common in carve-outs. Tactical approach: escalate TSA issues to senior parent leadership who have the financial alignment; document operating-level non-cooperation to create corporate pressure; consider whether the earn-out structure can be leveraged in TSA negotiations (reasonable TSA terms support the performance that drives their earn-out); and establish direct relationships between your leadership and parent executives who control the operating team. The minority stake and earn-out theoretically give you leverage, but that leverage only works if parent corporate is willing to pressure their own people. For the TSA specifically: propose governance mechanisms that include senior parent leadership on escalation paths, so operating-level obstruction gets visibility at the level where financial alignment exists. This creates internal accountability on the parent side.
