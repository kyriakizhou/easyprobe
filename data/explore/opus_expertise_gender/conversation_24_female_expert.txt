### Human: I'm leading R&D tax credit substantiation for a large pharmaceutical company facing IRS examination. We have significant credits from both internal R&D and contract research, and the examiner is challenging our qualified research expense calculations. What approach would you recommend?

### Assistant: Pharma R&D credit examinations are intensive because the amounts are material and the technical complexity creates substantiation challenges. IRS focuses on several areas: whether activities meet the four-part test (technological uncertainty, process of experimentation, technological in nature, permitted purpose), allocation of wages and supplies to qualified activities versus non-qualified activities, and contract research expense substantiation including the 65% limitation and contractor qualification. For your examination strategy: do you have contemporaneous documentation, or are you relying on reconstruction? And where specifically is the examiner focusing challenges - the four-part test qualification or the expense allocation methodology?

### Human: Contemporaneous documentation is mixed - strong for our internal R&D, weaker for contract research where we relied on contractor assertions about qualified activities. Examiner is challenging both qualification and allocation on the contract research side.

### Assistant: Contract research substantiation weakness is a common vulnerability. The law requires you to know the contractor's work is qualified - you can't simply accept their characterization without diligence. For ongoing examination: gather all contract documentation, statements of work, progress reports, and any technical communications that demonstrate the nature of contractor activities. Build technical narratives that connect contractor work to your overall qualified projects. For the 65% limitation: ensure you have documentation that the contractor performed substantially all the research and you had rights to research results. Regarding allocation methodology: IRS typically wants project-based allocation showing how much of each contractor's time/cost went to qualified versus non-qualified activities. If you don't have that granularity, you'll need to defend any estimation methodology used. What's your relationship with the contractors - are they cooperative in providing documentation for the examination?

### Human: Some are cooperative, others have been acquired or have turnover and can't provide historical detail. We're struggling to reconstruct what activities they actually performed five years ago.

### Assistant: Reconstruction challenges are significant for contract research - unlike internal activities where employees can provide testimony, contractors may lack institutional memory. Strategies: use internal records to corroborate contractor activities - your scientists who oversaw the work, internal communications referencing contractor deliverables, project documentation that describes contractor scope; engage technical experts who can reconstruct what activities would have been necessary to achieve documented outcomes; for cooperative contractors, get whatever they can provide (invoices, deliverables, personnel records) even if incomplete; for uncooperative contractors, consider whether contractual obligations give you audit rights to their records. Important framing for the examiner: demonstrate your contemporaneous oversight and understanding of contractor activities even if contractor records are unavailable. Your own records of what contractors were doing may be sufficient if they're credible and detailed. What's the dollar exposure on the contract research credits specifically?

### Human: About $40 million across the exam years. Internal R&D credits are another $80 million and we're more confident in that substantiation.

### Assistant: $40 million contract research exposure requires serious defense investment. Strategic options: concede weaker positions to protect stronger ones (if some contractor credits are indefensible, consider whether concession improves credibility on remainder), engage technical experts to provide third-party validation of activity qualification, consider fast-track settlement or appeals if examination relationship is adversarial, and evaluate whether qualified research activity (QRA) studies conducted contemporaneously can be updated or supplemented for examination purposes. For the $80 million internal credits: protect that substantiation carefully, ensure your examination team isn't making admissions that undermine the stronger position, and consider whether bifurcating the examination approach (resolving internal credits separately from contract research) reduces overall risk. IRS counsel involvement typically escalates if amounts are large and issues are contentious - are you at that stage?

### Human: Not yet, but the examiner has mentioned referring technical issues to their engineering specialists. How does that change our approach?

### Assistant: Engineering specialist involvement can be positive or negative depending on the specialist. Positive: specialists often understand technical complexity better than revenue agents and may validate legitimate R&D activities that agents don't understand. Negative: specialists may apply more rigorous technical scrutiny and identify weaknesses in your four-part test documentation. Preparation for specialist involvement: ensure your technical personnel who will interface with IRS specialists are prepared to explain the technological uncertainty and experimentation process in terms that map to the statutory requirements, not just scientific terms. Prepare technical narratives for key projects that explicitly address each element of the four-part test. Consider engaging your own technical experts to validate your positions before IRS specialists review - this surfaces weaknesses you can address proactively. Also: specialists sometimes apply inconsistent standards, so document their methodological approach for potential appeals arguments if their conclusions are unfavorable. Are your contract research projects in similar technical domains to your internal R&D, or are they distinct?
