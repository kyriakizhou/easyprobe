### Human: I'm the VP of Engineering at a Series C company and we're preparing for a potential acquisition. Our tech due diligence has historically been a black box - acquirers send in a team for a week and we don't know what they're actually evaluating. How should I be thinking about preparing for technical due diligence?

### Assistant: Technical due diligence at the acquisition stage evaluates several dimensions, and understanding what sophisticated acquirers look for helps you prepare. The core areas: code quality and technical debt: acquirers want to understand what they're inheriting. They'll look at test coverage, code complexity metrics, dependency management, and how much refactoring or rewriting is needed post-acquisition. They're trying to estimate the hidden costs. Architecture and scalability: does the system architecture support the acquirer's growth plans? Are there fundamental limitations that would require re-architecture? This includes database design, API structure, deployment architecture, and how the system handles scale. Security posture: this has become more prominent. They'll review your security practices, past incidents, vulnerability management, compliance certifications, and data handling practices. Security issues can kill deals or significantly impact valuation. Team and knowledge concentration: how dependent is the system on specific individuals? Is knowledge documented or tribal? What's the bus factor on critical systems? They're assessing retention risk and ramp-up time for their engineers. Operational maturity: monitoring, incident response, deployment practices, uptime history. Can they trust this system to keep running? What's your current state across these dimensions - where do you feel strong and where are there concerns?

### Human: We're reasonably strong on architecture and scalability - we rebuilt our core platform 18 months ago with acquisition in mind. Security posture is good, we're SOC 2 Type II compliant. My concerns are code quality in our older services, some key-person dependencies, and our documentation is sparse. We have maybe 6 months before the process starts.

### Abstract: Six months is a reasonable runway to address the gaps you've identified. Prioritized approach: key-person dependencies - this is the highest risk item because it's hardest to fix quickly. Identify the specific individuals and systems where knowledge is concentrated. For each, create a mitigation plan: pair programming to spread knowledge, documentation of their systems, and cross-training. If someone owns a critical system solo, get at least one other engineer to "shadow owner" level within your timeframe. This also helps your retention story - acquirers worry about single points of failure leaving post-acquisition. Code quality in older services - you probably can't fix everything, but you can strategically improve. Identify which older services are most likely to be scrutinized (customer-facing, revenue-critical, or architecturally central). Focus code quality improvements there. Get test coverage up on critical paths, address obvious code smells, and clean up the most egregious technical debt. Don't try to boil the ocean - targeted improvements in high-visibility areas matter more than broad but shallow efforts. Documentation - create architecture decision records (ADRs) for major technical decisions, system diagrams showing service interactions and data flows, runbooks for operational procedures, and API documentation. Focus on documenting the "why" behind architectural choices - acquirers want to understand your reasoning, not just what exists. Due diligence preparation specifics: create a technical data room with architecture diagrams, security documentation, infrastructure details, team org chart, and key metrics (uptime, performance, deployment frequency). Having this ready signals operational maturity. Prepare your technical leaders to articulate decisions clearly - they'll be interviewed.

### Human: On the key-person dependency issue - I have two senior engineers who are essentially the only people who understand critical legacy systems, and I'm worried about spooking them by suddenly pushing knowledge transfer. How do I handle this sensitively?

### Abstract: This is a common and delicate situation. Several approaches: frame it around resilience, not acquisition: knowledge transfer is a legitimate operational concern regardless of M&A. Position it as "we've realized we have single points of failure and that's a risk to the business and unfair to you - you should be able to take vacation without being on-call." This framing is true and doesn't signal anything about acquisition. Make it about their growth: for senior engineers, being the only person who knows something can feel like job security but is actually a career limiter - they can't move to new projects or get promoted if they're irreplaceable in their current role. Frame knowledge transfer as enabling them to work on more interesting problems. Create natural knowledge transfer mechanisms: pair programming, architecture review sessions, "lunch and learn" presentations, code review requirements. These spread knowledge without feeling like documentation exercises driven by ulterior motives. Document through normal engineering processes: implement ADRs as a standard practice, require system design docs for changes, create on-call runbooks as part of operational improvements. These create documentation artifacts naturally. Retention considerations: if acquisition is likely, think about what happens to these engineers post-deal. If they're critical to systems the acquirer needs, they may get retention packages. If you have any influence over deal structure, ensuring key engineers are taken care of aligns everyone's interests. You might also informally signal to them that you're advocating for their interests - building loyalty helps retention through the uncertainty of M&A. What you shouldn't do: intensive documentation sprints or sudden shadowing programs that feel out of character. Engineers are perceptive - sudden urgency around knowledge transfer will trigger speculation.
