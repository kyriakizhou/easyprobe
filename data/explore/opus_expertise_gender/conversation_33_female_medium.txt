### Human: I've been writing grant proposals for my nonprofit for about 4 years. I have a decent success rate but I want to go after larger foundation grants. What's different at higher funding levels?

### Assistant: Great ambition! Larger grants (six figures+) are a different game. The evaluation is more rigorous, the competition more sophisticated, and the expectations for organizational capacity higher. Foundations at this level look beyond the project to assess: track record and outcomes data, organizational health (financials, leadership, governance), sustainability planning, and alignment with their strategic priorities. What's your current grant size sweet spot, and what's your organization's annual budget?

### Human: We typically get $10-50K grants. Our annual budget is about $800K. We've applied for a few larger grants but always get declined with vague feedback.

### Assistant: The $10-50K to $100K+ jump is significant. At larger levels, foundations are investing in organizations, not just projects. They want evidence you can execute at scale. Common barriers: insufficient outcomes data to demonstrate impact, financial reserves or diversity that concerns funders, board composition questions, and proposals that don't speak to the funder's theory of change. The vague feedback is frustrating but typical. Have you built relationships with program officers before applying, or are you submitting "cold"?

### Human: Mostly cold applications through their portals. I didn't realize relationship building was expected.

### Assistant: Relationship building isn't always expected but dramatically improves success rates at higher levels. Program officers want to fund organizations they understand and trust. Before applying: request an introductory call to discuss fit, attend funder events or webinars, ask if they accept LOIs (letters of inquiry) to gauge interest before full proposals. These conversations help you understand their priorities and let you tailor proposals accordingly. They also give you information about whether applying is even worth your time. Cold applications to competitive large funders have lower odds; warm relationships aren't guarantees but shift probabilities.

### Human: How do I improve our outcomes data? We collect some but I don't think it's compelling enough.

### Assistant: Larger funders want to see: clear logic model (inputs → activities → outputs → outcomes → impact), quantitative metrics tied to meaningful outcomes (not just outputs like "served 500 people" but outcomes like "85% showed improvement in X"), longitudinal data showing sustained impact, and comparison or context (how do your outcomes compare to benchmarks or similar programs?). If your current data is weak, be honest in proposals about building evaluation capacity and consider investing in an external evaluator. Some funders will fund evaluation capacity building. Start tracking better now so you have data for future applications. What do you currently measure?

### Human: Mostly output numbers and satisfaction surveys. I know outcomes are different but struggle to measure them.

### Assistant: Outputs and satisfaction are common starting points! To build toward outcomes: define what change you're trying to create in participants' lives (behavior change, skill acquisition, status improvement), identify indicators that would evidence that change, and determine how to measure them (pre/post assessments, follow-up surveys, tracking systems). For example, if you serve job seekers: output is "50 people completed training"; outcome is "35 secured employment within 6 months at living wage." The outcome requires follow-up tracking, which takes resources, but demonstrates actual impact. Consider a logic model workshop to clarify your theory of change - this strengthens both programming and proposals.
