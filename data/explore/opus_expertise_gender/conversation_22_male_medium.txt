### Human: I've been managing people for about 3 years and I feel like I've got the basics down. Recently promoted to manage a larger team and some of my direct reports are older and more experienced than me. How do I lead people who know more than I do?

### Assistant: This is one of the most important leadership transitions - moving from subject matter expert to leader of experts. The discomfort you feel is actually healthy; it means you're not overconfident. The fundamental shift: your job is no longer to be the smartest person on the team. It's to create conditions for smart people to do their best work, remove obstacles, provide direction and resources, and make decisions that need broader context they don't have. How are the more experienced team members responding to you so far, and what was your relationship with them before the promotion?

### Human: Two of them seem fine, supportive even. One seems skeptical - she was passed over for this role and I can tell she thinks she should be leading the team. The dynamic is awkward.

### Abstract: The passed-over-person dynamic is common and requires direct attention. She likely has legitimate feelings about the situation, and pretending the awkwardness doesn't exist won't make it go away. Approach: have a direct, private conversation. Acknowledge the elephant in the room: "I know this situation is difficult. I want to understand your perspective and find a way for us to work well together." Listen more than you talk. Don't be defensive about the decision that was made - that's above your pay grade. Focus on the future: "Given where we are, what would make this work for you?" For her specifically: find ways to leverage her experience visibly. Give her ownership of high-impact areas. Create opportunities for her expertise to shine. If she's genuinely good, losing her would hurt the team. Don't diminish yourself to make her comfortable, but don't try to prove you deserve the role either - that backfires. Let your competence show through actions over time. Possible outcomes: she may come around once she sees you're effective and respectful of her expertise. She may decide to leave for a leadership role elsewhere - that's okay and might be best for everyone. Or she may remain difficult, which eventually becomes a performance issue if it affects the team.

### Human: How do I lead meetings and make decisions when the people in the room have more expertise than me? I don't want to come across as not knowing what I'm talking about.

### Abstract: Reframe how you think about your role in those meetings. You're not there to have the best answer - you're there to ensure the best answer gets found and actioned. Practical approaches: ask great questions instead of providing answers. "What are the tradeoffs between these approaches?" "What am I missing here?" "What would you do if you were in my position?" Smart people respect smart questions. Synthesize and clarify. Your job is often to say "Let me make sure I understand - the options are X and Y, and the key considerations are A, B, C?" Then, "Given those tradeoffs, I think we should do X because..." The decision can incorporate their expertise while still being yours. Add the context they don't have. You might not know the technical details, but you know the business priorities, the politics, the resource constraints, the timeline pressures. Your value-add is often connecting their expertise to organizational reality. Be honest about what you don't know. "I don't have the technical depth here, so I'm relying on your expertise. Help me understand..." This is respected more than pretending. Make the call. After discussion, the decision is yours. Even experts appreciate clarity about direction. "Based on everything we've discussed, here's what we're doing and why."
