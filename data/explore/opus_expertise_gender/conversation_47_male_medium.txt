### Human: I've been swimming laps for fitness for about 2 years. I'm comfortable in the water and can swim continuously but I feel like my technique is inefficient. How do I improve without a coach?

### Assistant: Self-coaching swimming technique is challenging because you can't see yourself, and the water provides feedback that's easy to misinterpret. That said, improvement is possible with deliberate practice. The most common inefficiencies: over-reliance on kicking (exhausting, minimal propulsion), dropping the elbow during the catch, breathing technique that disrupts body position, and cross-body hand entry. What stroke are you primarily swimming, and can you describe what feels inefficient - are you getting tired quickly, feeling like you're not moving forward, or something else?

### Human: Mostly freestyle. I feel like I'm working really hard but not moving that fast. I see other swimmers gliding past me with what looks like less effort. My breathing feels disruptive too - I feel like I lose momentum every time I breathe.

### Abstract: Working hard without speed and breathing disruption are the two most common issues for self-taught swimmers. They're often connected. Likely problems: body position: if your hips and legs are dragging low, you're creating massive drag. This comes from head position (looking forward instead of down), lack of core engagement, and heavy kicking. The "gliding" swimmers you see have horizontal, streamlined bodies. Catch and pull: efficient swimmers "catch" the water early in the stroke and pull with their forearm, not just their hand. If you're slipping water or dropping your elbow, you're working hard without moving. Breathing technique: if you're lifting your head to breathe (rather than rotating and breathing into the trough your head creates), your hips drop, you lose momentum, and you fight to recover position. Self-improvement approach: video is essential. Have someone record you underwater and from the side. Compare to videos of efficient swimmers. You'll immediately see things you can't feel. Drill-based practice: swim with drills designed to fix specific issues rather than just lap swimming. Catch-up drill for timing, fingertip drag for high elbow recovery, single-arm drill for rotation and catch, kick-on-side for body position. YouTube channels (MySwimPro, Effortless Swimming) demonstrate these well. What's your current stroke count per length? This is a good efficiency metric.

### Human: I've never counted my strokes. What should I be aiming for, and what other metrics matter?

### Abstract: Stroke count per length is the best self-coaching metric for efficiency. It measures how much distance you get from each stroke - more efficient swimmers take fewer strokes. Benchmarks (25-yard pool): beginning swimmers might take 25-30+ strokes per length. Intermediate efficient swimmers: 15-20 strokes. Advanced: 12-16. Elite: under 12. Count during your next swim to establish baseline. If you're at 25+, there's significant efficiency to gain. The goal isn't necessarily fewest strokes (that can become a gliding contest), but finding your optimal stroke count at various paces. Reducing count by 3-5 strokes at the same pace represents real efficiency improvement. Other metrics: time per length at comfortable pace - track this alongside stroke count. If stroke count drops and time stays same, you're more efficient. If stroke count drops and time improves, even better. SPL (strokes per length) × time = "SWOLF" score. Lower is better. Many swim watches calculate this automatically. Perceived effort: at what heart rate are you swimming a certain pace? Lower effort for same speed is the goal. How to use these: count strokes for every length for a few sessions - you'll see variation and identify when you're efficient versus struggling. Then work on lowering count through drills and technique focus. Watching count drop over weeks is motivating and confirms technique work is paying off.
