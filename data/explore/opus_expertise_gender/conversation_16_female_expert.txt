### Human: I'm restructuring a distressed multi-property hospitality portfolio. The capital structure includes first lien debt, mezz, preferred equity, and common - all with different sponsors who have conflicting interests. We're trying to negotiate a consensual restructuring but discussions are breaking down. What levers do I have?

### Assistant: Multi-tranche capital stack restructurings are inherently adversarial because the waterfall creates zero-sum dynamics. Each party's recovery depends on others' concessions. Your levers depend on where value breaks in the stack. First: what's your current valuation view relative to the debt layers, and what's driving the distress - operational (fixable) or structural (permanent impairment)?

### Human: We believe enterprise value is around $180M. First lien is $120M, mezz is $60M, preferred is $40M, common is $30M. Distress is primarily operational - RevPAR hasn't recovered post-COVID and we're burning cash, but we think value recovery is possible with time and capital.

### Assistant: At $180M EV, first lien is covered but underwater at current enterprise value, mezz is significantly impaired, and preferred/common are likely wiped out absent recovery. The interesting dynamic: first lien has collateral cushion but faces risk of further deterioration; mezz has most to fight for; preferred and common have option value on upside but no current recovery. Consensual restructuring requires: first lien accepting terms they might not need to accept (for speed and certainty), mezz accepting significant impairment, and junior capital contributing either new money or concessions to preserve any stake. What's blocking consensus - is it valuation disagreement, or allocation of new money requirements?

### Human: Both. First lien believes value is lower and they should control the process. Mezz believes value is higher and they should get meaningful recovery. Nobody wants to provide new money without control.

### Assistant: Classic standoff. Your tools: First, control the information flow - commission independent valuation to create shared fact base. If parties can't agree on value, structured solutions (equity participation, management rights, upside sharing) can bridge valuation gaps. Second, new money leverage - whoever provides liquidity has significant negotiating power. If you can find a third-party DIP provider or equity sponsor, you change the dynamics. Third, timeline pressure - if the business is deteriorating, delay costs everyone. Make cash burn projections explicit. Fourth, if consensual fails, you have process alternatives: first lien could credit bid in foreclosure, bankruptcy court can impose treatment (cramdown), or you can propose a 363 sale to a new sponsor. The threat of non-consensual process often motivates consensus.

### Human: First lien is threatening to foreclose and credit bid. How real is that threat?

### Assistant: Credit bid threat is real but has costs for first lien that give you leverage. They'd acquire the assets but: inherit operational problems they may not want to manage, lose mezz and junior cooperation and potentially face litigation, face time delays in the foreclosure process, and may not actually want to own hotels. Test their conviction: are they real estate operators or debt investors? Debt investors often prefer negotiated outcome over asset ownership. Also: depending on jurisdiction and documentation, mezz and other parties may have rights that complicate foreclosure - review your intercreditor agreements carefully. Sometimes the threat of messy contested foreclosure motivates first lien to negotiate more reasonably. What's the first lien holder's profile - bank, CLO, distressed fund?

### Human: It's a CMBS special servicer, which complicates decision-making.

### Assistant: Special servicer dynamics change everything. Special servicers: have fiduciary duty to the trust, not economic optimization for any party; are constrained by PSA (pooling and servicing agreement) terms; often prefer status quo over complex restructuring that creates liability; and may have conflicts if they control the workout fee or have B-piece or other economic interests. Negotiating with special servicers: understand their incentive structure, provide well-documented analysis they can use to justify decisions to the trust, make proposals that fit within their authority, and be patient - decision cycles are longer. The good news: special servicers are often willing to extend and pretend, which buys time. The bad news: they're not nimble partners in complex consensual deals. Consider whether a bondholder group could direct or replace the special servicer if current approach isn't working.
