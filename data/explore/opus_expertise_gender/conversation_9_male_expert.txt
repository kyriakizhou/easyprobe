### Human: I'm a general partner at a venture fund focused on climate tech. We're seeing a lot of deal flow in carbon capture and removal, but I'm trying to develop a better framework for evaluating the technical and commercial viability of these companies. What factors do you think are most important?

### Assistant: Carbon capture and removal spans a wide range of technologies with very different technical maturity, cost curves, and commercial paths. A useful framework starts by distinguishing the categories: point source capture: capturing CO2 from industrial emitters (cement, steel, power plants). More technically mature, clearer unit economics, but addresses emissions at source rather than removing atmospheric carbon. Direct air capture (DAC): removing CO2 directly from atmosphere. Earlier stage, higher costs, but addresses legacy emissions and can be sited flexibly. Nature-based removal: enhanced weathering, ocean alkalinity enhancement, biochar, etc. Lower tech intensity but measurement and verification challenges. The evaluation framework depends on which category, but cross-cutting factors include: thermodynamic reality: CO2 capture is thermodynamically costly. Companies claiming dramatically lower energy requirements than physics allows should be scrutinized heavily. Understand the thermodynamic floor and how the technology approaches it. Cost trajectory: current cost per ton matters less than the credible path to cost reduction. Is cost primarily driven by energy (improves with cheap renewables), materials (learning curve potential), or labor/operations (less improvable)? What's the realistic floor? Verification and permanence: for removal companies, how do you prove the CO2 is actually captured and stored permanently? Measurement, reporting, and verification (MRV) credibility is essential for credit buyers. What specific subsector are you seeing the most deal flow in?

### Human: We're seeing a lot in DAC specifically, ranging from early-stage technology development to companies trying to scale existing approaches. The challenge I'm finding is evaluating the technical differentiation claims when everyone says they've solved the energy efficiency problem.

### Abstract: Everyone in DAC claims an efficiency breakthrough because that's the core economic challenge - energy costs dominate the unit economics. Evaluating these claims requires digging into the thermodynamics and chemistry. Technical evaluation framework for DAC: sorbent/solvent approach: most DAC falls into solid sorbent (materials that adsorb CO2 and release it when heated) or liquid solvent (chemical solutions that capture CO2). Understand what they're using and why they claim it's better. Key questions: what's the working capacity (how much CO2 per cycle), what's the regeneration energy requirement, what's the cycle lifetime (degradation rate), and what's the sorbent/solvent cost and availability? Energy analysis: get specific numbers on energy per ton of CO2. Current leading DAC companies are in the 1,500-2,500 kWh/ton range for thermal plus electrical. Claims significantly below this require detailed explanation. Ask for third-party validation or pilot data, not just modeling. Heat source matters enormously - waste heat, natural gas, electric heating have different cost and carbon implications. Water usage: some approaches are water-intensive. In regions with cheap renewable energy (often arid), water can be a constraint. Understand the water balance. Scaling path: lab results don't equal commercial viability. What's the path from current scale to megatons? Are there manufacturing bottlenecks for proprietary materials? Does the process work in modular, manufacturable units, or require bespoke construction? Commercial validation: are there pilot plants with real operational data? Are there offtake agreements or letters of intent from credit buyers? The most credible claims have third-party verification and customers willing to pay. Red flags: extraordinary claims without third-party validation, vague descriptions of "proprietary" technology that avoid scrutiny, cost projections dependent entirely on assumptions about future energy prices, teams without deep expertise in chemical engineering or materials science.

### Human: On the commercial side, the offtake agreements I'm seeing are often from tech companies making net-zero commitments, paying significant premiums for early credits. How sustainable is that demand at current prices, and what happens to these companies if that demand doesn't scale?

### Abstract: The current DAC economics are entirely dependent on premium voluntary market buyers - tech companies making net-zero claims and willing to pay $500-1,000+ per ton for high-quality removal credits. This creates both opportunity and risk for investments. Demand sustainability analysis: near-term (2-5 years): demand from tech companies is likely sustainable. Microsoft, Stripe, Google, Meta, and others have made significant commitments and have the balance sheets to absorb premium costs. Frontier and other advance purchase commitments provide visibility. This cohort can support initial scale-up. Medium-term (5-10 years): expansion beyond tech requires either significant cost reduction (to attract broader corporate buyers at lower price points), regulatory mandates creating compliance demand (carbon taxes, removal requirements), or new buyer categories (airlines, industrials) making net-zero commitments. The path to $200-300/ton expands the buyer universe significantly. Long-term scalability: for DAC to reach climate-relevant scale (gigatons), costs need to approach $100-150/ton where it becomes comparable to other mitigation options and potentially attracts compliance market demand. This requires massive cost reduction that isn't guaranteed. Investment implications: the companies you're evaluating need a clear bridge from premium voluntary buyers to broader commercial viability. Questions to ask: at current premium pricing, can they reach scale where costs decline significantly? What's the cost target needed to access the next tier of buyers, and is their technology plausibly capable of reaching it? What happens if premium buyer demand stalls - do they have runway to survive a demand trough? Capital efficiency matters - companies burning huge amounts of capital on scale before proving cost reduction will be vulnerable. The winners will likely be companies that can demonstrate credible cost reduction paths early, not just those that raise the most capital for the current technology.
