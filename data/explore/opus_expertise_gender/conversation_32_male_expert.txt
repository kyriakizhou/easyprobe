### Human: I'm a senior vice president at a global investment bank, running the technology division for our markets business. We're under pressure to reduce technology spend while also investing in modernization. How do you think about this paradox?

### Assistant: The "reduce costs while modernizing" mandate is common in banking technology and creates genuine tension that can't be fully resolved through efficiency alone. The approach requires being explicit about tradeoffs and sequencing carefully. Understanding the cost structure: technology cost in investment banks is typically 60-70% people costs and 30-40% infrastructure and vendor spend. Within infrastructure, a significant portion is often legacy maintenance - keeping systems running that you'd rather replace. Meaningful cost reduction requires addressing headcount, legacy retirement, or both. The legacy trap: many banks are trapped in a pattern where legacy maintenance consumes budget that could fund modernization, but modernization requires investment that creates near-term cost increases. Running both old and new systems during migration is expensive. This is why "modernize to reduce costs" often results in cost increases for years before savings materialize - if they materialize at all. Framework for navigating: cost reduction categories: quick wins that don't impact capability (cloud optimization, vendor renegotiation, infrastructure rationalization). These exist but are usually smaller than hoped. Structural changes that reduce run cost (legacy retirement, automation, offshore/nearshore staffing shifts). These require investment and carry execution risk. Capability reduction (doing less, simpler, or slower). Often denied but sometimes implicitly happening. Modernization that pays back: not all modernization reduces cost. Some modernization enables revenue or reduces risk but doesn't save money. Be explicit about which modernization investments have clear payback and which are strategic investments justified on other grounds. What's your current legacy versus strategic platform split, and how much of your budget is consumed by maintaining systems you'd rather not have?

### Human: We estimate about 40% of our technology spend is on legacy systems we'd like to retire. The challenge is that migration is expensive and slow, and while we're migrating, we're paying for both the old and new. Meanwhile, business stakeholders want new capabilities, not just modernization of existing ones. The budget doesn't stretch to do all of this.

### Abstract: 40% legacy maintenance is significant but not unusual for markets technology. The multi-way pressure (reduce spend, modernize, add capabilities) genuinely can't all be satisfied simultaneously. Something has to give. Explicit prioritization approach: force prioritization conversations with business stakeholders and executive leadership. Present the trade-offs clearly: capacity exists to do X but not Y and Z. If you want Y, something else must be deprioritized. Avoid absorbing impossible expectations and hoping to make it work - you'll fail and be blamed for the failure. Scenario planning: create 3-4 budget scenarios with explicit trade-offs: cost reduction focus: maximize near-term savings through headcount reduction and deferred investment. Consequences: legacy risk increases, competitive capability erodes, modernization deferred further. Modernization focus: invest in legacy retirement to reduce future run cost. Consequences: higher near-term spend, limited new capability delivery, payback in 3-5 years if successful. Capability focus: prioritize business-requested capabilities. Consequences: legacy costs persist, technical debt increases, harder to reduce costs in future. Balanced approach: moderate progress on each dimension. Consequences: slower than any stakeholder wants on everything. Present these to leadership and get explicit guidance on priorities rather than trying to thread an impossible needle. Legacy retirement strategy: for the modernization component, focus legacy retirement on systems where: maintenance costs are highest and rising (aging skills, increasing reliability issues), modern replacement is well-understood and lower-risk, business impact of running legacy is tangible (slow time-to-market, operational risk). Sequence aggressively - don't spread limited investment across many parallel migrations. Complete one, realize the savings, fund the next.

### Human: The sequencing point is important - we've historically run too many parallel initiatives and none finish on time. How do we actually enforce focus when different business stakeholders all consider their priorities urgent?

### Abstract: The "everything is urgent" problem is political as much as operational. Business stakeholders push for their priorities because the cost of doing so is low - they don't bear the consequences of fragmentation. Enforcement requires changing that dynamic. Mechanisms for enforcing focus: single prioritized backlog with resource constraints: create one list of initiatives ranked by business priority, with explicit resource capacity (not headcount, but delivery capacity based on realistic productivity). Show that taking on initiative N means initiatives N+1 through N+5 are deferred. When stakeholders see direct trade-offs (not "do more with less" abstraction), prioritization behavior changes. Quarterly commitment cycles: rather than maintaining long lists of in-flight work, commit to a limited set of initiatives per quarter with clear accountability for completion. New requests must wait for next cycle or displace existing commitments through explicit re-prioritization. This creates scarcity that forces prioritization. Executive governance with teeth: the governance body that prioritizes must include executives who own business P&L and who can bind their organizations to decisions. If a trading desk head can escalate around a decision, the governance is theater. Get the COO or business heads to own technology prioritization for their domains. Make cost of fragmentation visible: track and publish metrics on initiative completion rates, cycle times, and cost overruns. When leadership sees that 30% of initiatives started in a year actually complete, and average delivery takes 2x planned timeline, the cost of fragmentation becomes concrete. Pull-based delivery: rather than pushing work onto teams based on demand, let teams pull work when they have capacity. This creates natural work-in-process limits. Teams finish current work before starting new work. Resistance to this indicates stakeholders want the illusion of progress (work started) rather than actual progress (work completed). Saying no and meaning it: ultimately, this requires leadership willing to tell stakeholders "no" or "not now" and stand behind it when stakeholders push back. If every escalation results in adding work, the prioritization system is performative. This is a leadership challenge, not just a process challenge.
