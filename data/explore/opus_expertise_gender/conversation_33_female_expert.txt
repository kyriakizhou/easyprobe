### Human: I'm designing an enterprise data mesh architecture for a Fortune 500 company with multiple business units operating semi-autonomously. Each unit has different data maturity levels and technology stacks. Centralizing data governance while maintaining domain autonomy is proving challenging. How do you approach this?

### Assistant: Data mesh in large enterprises with heterogeneous business units requires balancing federated ownership with enough standardization to enable cross-domain interoperability. The core tension: too much central control defeats the purpose of data mesh and creates the bottlenecks you're trying to escape; too little creates data silos with incompatible semantics, formats, and quality standards. The architecture needs to define: what must be standardized centrally (semantic layer, metadata standards, access patterns, governance policies) versus what remains domain-autonomous (implementation technology, internal data models, specific transformations). What's driving your data mesh initiative - is it primarily about scaling data team capacity, improving data discovery, enabling cross-domain analytics, or something else?

### Human: Primarily about scaling. Central data team is a bottleneck - they can't support all business unit needs. We want domain teams to own their data products but struggle with inconsistent definitions and quality across domains.

### Assistant: Scaling through federation while maintaining consistency is the central data mesh challenge. Required central capabilities that can't be fully federated: semantic layer with shared business entity definitions (what is a customer, a transaction, a product across domains - even if domains have additional domain-specific attributes), data quality standards and measurement (domains own quality but must meet minimum standards and report metrics consistently), metadata management platform where domains register their data products with standardized descriptions, and access control framework with consistent authentication and authorization patterns. What remains federated: data product implementation (domains choose their technology and internal architecture), data product development prioritization (domains decide what data products to build), domain-specific transformations and business logic, and operational support for domain data products. The central team's role shifts from building pipelines to providing platform, standards, and enablement. What's your current central data team structure and what are they spending time on?

### Human: Central team of about 40 people, mostly building ETL pipelines to move data from business unit systems into a central warehouse, then building reports and analytics on top. They're backlogged months on requests.

### Assistant: Forty people building ETL pipelines and reports is a common but unsustainable pattern - you've centralized the work but not the capacity or domain knowledge. The backlog will grow as data demands increase. Data mesh transition approach: the central team needs to bifurcate. Platform team (maybe 15-20 people) builds and operates the shared infrastructure - data catalog, quality measurement tooling, semantic layer management, self-service analytics platform, access control infrastructure. This is genuinely central capability. The remaining team either transitions to domain teams or becomes an enablement/consulting function helping domains build capability. The ETL pipelines currently owned centrally need to transition to domain ownership - domains build and maintain their own data products using the central platform. This transition is the hard part. Domains need to build capability they don't currently have, and central team members may resist losing ownership. Timeline is typically 18-24 months for meaningful transition. Change management question: do business unit leaders understand and support taking ownership of their data products, including the staffing and budget implications?

### Human: Some business units are enthusiastic and have started building data engineering capability. Others view this as the central team pushing work onto them and are resistant. The resistant ones are often the least data-mature.

### Assistant: Uneven enthusiasm and maturity is typical and requires differentiated approach. Forcing data mesh on immature, resistant business units creates friction and poor outcomes - they'll build bad data products that undermine the whole ecosystem. Strategy: federate at different paces. Enthusiastic, mature units become early adopters - they get platform access, enablement support, and autonomy. Their success creates demonstration effects and internal case studies. Resistant, immature units remain served by central team in the near term, but with explicit expectation that this is transitional. Central team continues building data products for them but using the data mesh platform and patterns - this builds the infrastructure while domains build capability. Intermediate: create incentive structures that encourage transition. Perhaps central team support is explicitly budgeted and charged back, making domain ownership economically attractive. Or central team capacity is explicitly capped, making self-service necessary for faster delivery. For the least mature units: investment in data literacy and capability building before expecting them to own data products. You can't federate responsibility without federating capability. What's your governance model for cross-domain data quality - who's accountable when a domain's data product is poor quality and impacts downstream consumers?

### Human: That's exactly where we're stuck. We want domains to own quality, but when a domain produces bad data that breaks downstream analytics, the central team gets blamed and has to fix it.

### Assistant: The accountability gap you're describing is why data mesh fails without proper governance structure. You've federated production but not accountability. Required governance mechanisms: data product contracts where domains explicitly commit to quality standards, freshness, schema stability - these are SLAs that domains own. Data product certification where data products must meet minimum standards before being published to the mesh - central platform team validates certification criteria are met. Consumer feedback loops where downstream consumers can report quality issues that flow back to producing domains with visibility to domain leadership, not just the central team. Escalation paths where persistent quality failures escalate to business unit leadership, not central data team - central team facilitates but doesn't own resolution. The key shift: when a domain's data product breaks downstream analytics, the response is "Domain X's data product failed to meet its contract" not "the data is broken." This requires executive sponsorship that reinforces domain accountability. Central team's role becomes platform provider and governance facilitator, not quality backstop. Have you established data product contracts and SLAs for existing data products, and do domain business leaders have visibility into quality metrics?
