### Human: I've been baking bread at home for about two years and I'm comfortable with basic sourdough. But my crumb is inconsistent - sometimes I get nice open holes, sometimes it's tight and gummy. What am I doing wrong?

### Assistant: Inconsistent crumb usually points to fermentation or shaping issues, and getting consistent results is the real challenge of bread baking! Let me ask: are you tracking bulk fermentation by time or by dough development? And what's your hydration percentage typically?

### Human: I usually go by time - about 4-5 hours at room temperature. Hydration is around 72-75%. My starter is active and usually doubles in 4-6 hours.

### Assistant: Going by time is likely your main issue! Fermentation speed varies dramatically based on room temperature, flour, and starter activity. On warm days you might be over-proofed by 4 hours; on cold days, under-proofed. Switch to watching the dough: look for 50-75% rise in bulk, domed surface with some bubbles, and when you do an aliquot jar test or poke the dough, it should spring back slowly but not completely. A tight, gummy crumb often means under-proofed; giant irregular holes with no structure can mean over-proofed.

### Human: I've heard about the aliquot jar method but never tried it. How exactly does it work?

### Assistant: It's a game-changer for consistency! When you start bulk fermentation, put a small amount of dough in a straight-sided jar and mark the starting level with a rubber band. As your main dough ferments, the aliquot jar rises too, giving you a visual percentage. When the jar shows 50-75% rise (depending on flour strength and your preference), bulk is done. It removes the guesswork and accounts for environmental variation. Some bakers even track temperature and times to build a personal database of what works. Total-Dough-Feeling eventually develops, but the jar accelerates learning.

### Human: What about shaping? Could that affect the crumb too?

### Assistant: Absolutely! Shaping creates surface tension that allows the dough to expand upward rather than outward, which affects crumb structure. If shaping is too loose, you get a flat loaf with dense crumb. Too aggressive, and you degas the dough, losing those open holes. The goal is firm surface tension while preserving internal air bubbles. Practice your preshape-rest-final shape sequence. Let the dough rest long enough between stages (20-30 minutes) so it relaxes. Also check your flour - higher protein bread flour holds structure better than AP flour at high hydration.

### Human: I'm using King Arthur Bread Flour. Is that good enough?

### Assistant: KA Bread Flour is excellent - consistently high protein (around 12.7%) and strong gluten development. That's not your issue! With good flour and an active starter, focus on: 1) Reading fermentation rather than timing it, 2) Maintaining consistent dough temperature (78-80°F is ideal for predictable fermentation), and 3) Gentle but effective shaping. Consider getting a probe thermometer to track dough temp. Also, keep notes! Record every bake - room temp, bulk time, dough temp, results. Patterns will emerge. Bread baking rewards observation and adjustment over rigid recipes.
