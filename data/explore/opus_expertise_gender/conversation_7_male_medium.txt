### Human: I've been playing acoustic guitar for about 5 years, mostly fingerstyle and singer-songwriter stuff. I want to get into jazz but the chord voicings and improvisation seem overwhelming. Where do I start?

### Assistant: Jazz guitar is a big shift from singer-songwriter acoustic, but five years of fingerstyle gives you a solid technical foundation. The overwhelming feeling is normal - jazz has its own vocabulary that takes time to internalize. The good news: you don't need to learn everything at once. Most jazz guitar education throws too much at beginners - 50 chord voicings, modes, bebop scales, standards. Start narrower. The core building blocks: major 7, minor 7, and dominant 7 chord shapes (just 2-3 voicings each to start), and the major scale patterns that let you navigate the fretboard. Are you planning to stay acoustic or get an archtop/electric?

### Human: I'm staying acoustic for now. I don't want to invest in new gear until I know I'll stick with it. What should I actually practice?

### Abstract: Acoustic works fine for learning - Django Reinhardt played acoustic his whole career. Here's a focused starting path: learn the basic ii-V-I progression in a few keys. This is the most common jazz chord movement. In C: Dm7 - G7 - Cmaj7. Learn to play this with basic voicings, then with different voicings, then in different keys. This single progression teaches you most of what you need to know about jazz harmony. Start learning one standard thoroughly rather than many superficially. "Autumn Leaves" is the classic starter - the changes are approachable and it's called at every jam session. Learn the melody, the chord changes, and practice playing the chords while singing or humming the melody. For improvisation: start with the chord tones only. Over each chord, play only the 1-3-5-7 of that chord. This sounds musical immediately and teaches you to hear the harmony. Add scale notes later. Practice with backing tracks - iReal Pro or YouTube "Autumn Leaves backing track" will give you something to play along with.

### Human: I've heard about modes a lot but I don't understand how they apply. Is that something I need to learn early?

### Abstract: Modes are over-emphasized in early jazz education, honestly. Here's the reality: yes, theoretically, over a Dm7 chord you're playing D Dorian, over G7 you're playing G Mixolydian. But thinking that way while improvising is like trying to speak a language by thinking about grammar mid-sentence. More practically: in a ii-V-I in C, you can often just use the C major scale over all three chords and sound fine. The chord tones (1-3-5-7 of each chord) handle the heavy lifting of outlining the harmony - the other scale notes are color. My suggestion: don't study modes as a theory topic right now. Instead, learn to hear the chord tones, practice targeting chord tones on strong beats, and let the "modal" knowledge emerge naturally from playing. When you play the C major scale over Dm7 and emphasize D-F-A-C, you're playing Dorian whether you know it or not. After 6-12 months of playing over changes, modes will make more sense as a descriptive tool rather than a prescriptive one. The players who really know modes didn't learn them from a book - they learned them from playing.
