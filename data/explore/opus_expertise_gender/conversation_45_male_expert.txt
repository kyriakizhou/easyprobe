### Human: I'm the executive director of a mid-size foundation (about $500M in assets). We're rethinking our grantmaking strategy, specifically whether to continue our current diversified approach or concentrate in fewer areas with larger grants. What framework would you use for this decision?

### Assistant: The concentration versus diversification debate in philanthropy involves genuine tradeoffs, and the right answer depends on your foundation's theory of change, risk tolerance, and organizational capabilities. Neither approach is inherently superior. Framework for evaluating the decision: theory of change clarity: concentrated grantmaking works best when you have a clear theory of change - you understand the problem, the intervention, and the pathway to impact. If your understanding is incomplete, concentration risks putting significant resources behind a flawed approach. Diversification provides more learning opportunities but may prevent reaching the scale needed for systemic impact. Current portfolio assessment: before deciding on strategy, assess current performance. Are your diversified grants generating meaningful impact, or spreading resources too thin to matter? Is your foundation developing deep expertise in grant areas, or remaining superficial across many? Is your administrative burden manageable? Honest assessment of current state informs the direction of change. Organizational capacity: concentrated grantmaking requires deep expertise in focus areas. Do you have program staff with genuine domain knowledge? Can you attract that talent? Diversified approaches require breadth but less depth per area. Match strategy to organizational capability or invest in building the capability you need. Field maturity: in mature fields with established interventions and organizations, additional grantmaking may have diminishing returns unless you can fund at scale. In emerging fields, smaller grants to multiple approaches may surface what works. Match your approach to field development stage. What's driving the reconsideration - are you seeing limitations with the current diversified approach, or is this a proactive strategic review?

### Human: We've been diversified across six program areas for about 15 years. We have capable program officers but I'm not sure we're achieving impact commensurate with our grantmaking. Our grants average about $200K, and I wonder if we're spreading ourselves too thin to actually move the needle in any area. But I'm also wary of putting all our eggs in fewer baskets.

### Abstract: Your concern about being spread too thin is common for foundations at your scale. $500M in assets generates roughly $20-25M in annual grantmaking (at standard 5% payout). Across six program areas with $200K average grants, that's perhaps 100-120 grants across six areas - roughly 15-20 grants per area. That's enough to have a portfolio but may not be enough for field-shaping influence in any single area. Assessment framework: per-area influence capacity: in each of your six areas, assess your relative position. Are you among the largest funders, or a small player? Can your grantmaking actually shift outcomes, or are you supplementing larger funders' work? In areas where you're marginal, either concentrate more resources or exit. Distinctive contribution: where does your foundation add value beyond dollars? Do you have unique expertise, convening power, or strategic perspective in certain areas? Concentration should favor areas where you have distinctive contribution potential. Grantee perspective: talk to grantees about optimal grant size. Many nonprofit leaders prefer fewer, larger grants to many small ones - administrative burden is high per grant relationship. Your $200K average may be meaningful for some grantees and trivial for others. Field dynamics: in some fields, $3-4M annually (concentrated in one area) could make you an influential funder. In others, that's still marginal. Assess where concentration would actually achieve influence. Transition considerations: abrupt exit from program areas disrupts grantees and wastes accumulated learning. Any transition should be phased, with clear communication to current grantees and commitment to honoring existing relationships. Practical path: rather than binary concentration versus diversification, consider a middle path: maintain reduced portfolio in some areas (perhaps 3-4 instead of 6), with deeper investment in 1-2 areas where you can be truly influential. This provides some diversification while enabling meaningful concentration.

### Human: If we do concentrate, how do we decide which areas to prioritize? We have historical commitments and program staff expertise in all six areas, and I'm worried about the political dynamics of choosing some areas over others.

### Abstract: Area prioritization is both an analytical and political challenge. The analytical framework provides rationale; the process design manages politics. Analytical prioritization criteria: strategic fit: which areas best align with your foundation's mission, values, and theory of change? If founders or board have particular commitments, those carry weight. Marginal impact potential: where can additional concentration have the greatest impact relative to current investment? This combines field need, your foundation's potential influence, and evidence quality for interventions. Organizational capability: where do you have strongest staff expertise and networks? Concentration in areas where you lack capability requires hiring or partnership. Complementarity: how do other funders cover these areas? Concentrating where others are already deeply invested may have less marginal value than areas that are underfunded relative to need. Track record: which of your current programs show evidence of impact? Be honest about where you've succeeded and where results are unclear. Process for managing politics: board-led strategic process: frame this as a strategic review led by the board, not a staff reorganization. The board makes strategy decisions based on staff input. This depersonalizes the decision from individual program officers. External facilitation: consider engaging external consultants to facilitate the review. External perspectives can provide cover for difficult decisions and bring analytical rigor that internal discussions may lack. Inclusive process with clear decision authority: involve program staff in analysis and deliberation, but be clear that the board makes final decisions. Staff input is valued; staff don't have veto. Honoring transitions: if areas are eliminated or reduced, commit to transition support - for grantees (multi-year phase-out grants) and for staff (professional development, time to find new roles, severance if needed). Generous transition treatment reduces resistance. Reframe the narrative: rather than "we're cutting programs," frame as "we're focusing resources where we can have greatest impact." Program officers in continuing areas should feel empowered, not guilty. Those in transitioning areas should feel appreciated, not discarded. Timeline: don't rush. A thoughtful 6-12 month strategic review process builds legitimacy. Hasty decisions feel political; deliberate processes feel principled.
