For XVA specifically, they're focused on: model governance and documentation, independent validation rigor, understanding of model limitations and estimation uncertainty, backtesting and benchmarking, and the interplay between XVA models and front-office pricing. Calibration choices, netting assumptions, and margin period of risk assumptions all materially impact output. FVA and KVA - these are less standardized than CVA, and regulatory expectations around methodology and validation are still evolving. ### Human: We had some exam findings around our CVA model validation - specifically around the independence of validation, the comprehensiveness of our backtesting, and documentation of model limitations. ### Abstract: Exam findings in those specific areas are common and addressable, but they often reveal deeper infrastructure limitations that are harder to fix. Addressing the specific findings: independence of validation: regulators want clear separation between model development and validation. If the same quantitative team builds and validates, that's a problem. For regional banks without large quant teams, options include: dedicated model validation function (even if small) with clear reporting lines separate from trading, use of external validators for XVA models specifically (consultancies or vendor validation services), or clearly documented validation frameworks with challenge requirements that create effective independence even if organizational structure is limited. Backtesting comprehensiveness: CVA backtesting is genuinely difficult because you're predicting exposure distributions over long horizons and most trades don't default. Regulators want to see: exposure model backtesting (comparing predicted exposures to realized), P&L attribution analysis showing XVA contributions, and sensitivity-based analysis where appropriate. You may need to expand your backtesting framework to cover more dimensions than you currently do. Documentation of model limitations: this is often the easiest to address. Create explicit documentation of: model assumptions and where they break down, parameter estimation uncertainty and sensitivity, known approximations and their materiality, conditions under which models perform poorly. Regulators want to see that you understand your models' weaknesses. The deeper infrastructure question: if your XVA infrastructure is dated, tactical fixes to validation may not be sustainable. ### Human: It's primarily a vendor solution that we've heavily customized over the years. The customizations have created technical debt and make it difficult to upgrade. We're debating whether to invest in modernization or continue patching. What factors should drive that decision?

### Abstract: The modernization versus patching decision is fundamentally a cost-benefit analysis complicated by significant uncertainty on both sides. Factors to evaluate: regulatory trajectory: if you project that regulatory requirements will continue increasing (they will), assess whether your current architecture can accommodate future requirements with patches, or whether you'll face repeated investment cycles. Patching today that becomes obsolete with next year's requirements is expensive. Business strategy: is your derivatives business growing, stable, or contracting? If you're planning to grow XVA-relevant products, investment in modern infrastructure has greater payoff. If derivatives is a legacy business you're managing down, patching may be appropriate. Total cost of ownership: compare honestly the cost of continued patching (development time, operational risk, validation effort for customizations, opportunity cost of quant time spent on maintenance) versus modernization (vendor licensing, implementation, parallel running, revalidation). The cost of patching tends to be underestimated because it accumulates gradually. Operational risk: heavily customized legacy systems create operational risk - key person dependency, difficulty testing changes, integration fragility. Quantify incidents and near-misses related to current infrastructure complexity. Vendor direction: is your current vendor investing in their platform, or is it legacy for them too? If they're moving resources to a new platform, your customization technical debt will compound as their support quality declines. The modernization decision framework: if the present value of patching costs (including operational risk) over a reasonable horizon exceeds modernization costs, modernize. But be realistic about modernization costs - implementation, parallel running, and revalidation for XVA models is substantial. I've seen projects budget 2 years and take 4. Also consider a phased approach - can you modernize components incrementally rather than big-bang replacement? This reduces risk but may not fully solve integration complexity.
