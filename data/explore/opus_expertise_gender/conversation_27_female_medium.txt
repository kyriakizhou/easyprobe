### Human: I work as a therapist and I'm comfortable with individual sessions, but I've been asked to facilitate group therapy and it feels like a completely different skill set. How do I adapt?

### Assistant: You're right that it's a different skill set - individual and group therapy share foundations but require different competencies. In individual work, you manage one relationship; in groups, you manage multiple relationships plus group dynamics. The therapeutic factors are also different: in groups, peer support, universality, and interpersonal learning become central. What type of group would you be facilitating - process group, psychoeducational, support?

### Human: It would be a process group for anxiety, probably 8-10 members. Weekly sessions. I've observed groups but never led one.

### Assistant: Process groups are rich but demanding! Key shifts from individual work: you're facilitating relationships between members, not just with you. Your role is to create conditions for therapeutic work to happen among members. Balance intervening with allowing organic group process. For anxiety specifically, the group itself can trigger anxiety (exposure!) which becomes therapeutic material. Early sessions: establish norms, build safety, help members connect. Later: step back more, encourage member-to-member interaction. Resist the pull to be the expert who addresses each person individually in sequence - that's just parallel individual therapy.

### Human: What about difficult dynamics - like if one person dominates or there's conflict between members?

### Assistant: Anticipate these! Dominators are common - often anxious themselves and filling space. Strategies: reflect the pattern to the group ("I notice Susan has been sharing a lot - I wonder what others are thinking"), actively invite quieter members, and privately meet with dominators to explore what's happening. Conflict between members is actually therapeutically valuable if handled well - it's real interpersonal material. Don't rescue too quickly. Instead, slow it down, encourage both parties to express experience, and help the group witness and learn. Your job isn't preventing discomfort but helping the group work through it productively. Pre-group screening helps identify who's group-ready.

### Human: Screening - what should I be looking for?

### Assistant: Screening prevents problems. Red flags for group readiness: severe personality disorder features that would monopolize or alienate, active crisis or instability, severe social anxiety that would make participation torturous, or goals incompatible with group process. Good candidates show: motivation to work on interpersonal issues, basic capacity to share and listen, some distress tolerance, and willingness to give and receive feedback. During screening, explain group norms and expectations clearly, ask about their history in groups, and assess their understanding of confidentiality obligations. Trust your clinical judgment - if someone seems like they'd derail the group, it's okay to defer or refer.

### Human: Any resources specifically for therapists transitioning to group work?

### Assistant: Excellent resources: Irvin Yalom's "The Theory and Practice of Group Psychotherapy" is the classic text (5th edition with Leszcz). For anxiety groups specifically, there's growing literature on transdiagnostic group CBT. AGPA (American Group Psychotherapy Association) offers training and consultation. Many clinicians benefit from co-facilitating with an experienced group therapist initially - the mentorship is invaluable. Also consider your own experience as a group member - personal growth groups or T-groups give you insider understanding of member experience. Consultation with a group-experienced colleague as you start can help you process what's happening and refine your approach.
