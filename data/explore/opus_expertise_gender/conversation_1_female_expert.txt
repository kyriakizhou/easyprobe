### Human: We're seeing significant degradation in our recommendation model performance over the past quarter - offline metrics still look good but A/B tests show declining lift. Classic training-serving skew symptoms but we've checked the obvious pipeline issues. What else should we be investigating?

### Assistant: Training-serving skew is often blamed but the "offline looks good, online looks bad" pattern can have subtler causes. A few directions: feature distribution drift between training and serving that your offline validation isn't catching, temporal leakage in your training data that inflates offline metrics, or changes in user behavior that your model hasn't adapted to. What's your retraining cadence and how are you constructing your offline evaluation sets?

### Human: Weekly retraining, offline eval uses a held-out time window from the previous week. We're tracking feature distributions and haven't seen major drift in the monitored features.

### Assistant: Weekly retraining with recent holdout should catch gradual drift, so that's less likely. A few areas to probe: are you monitoring the right features? Sometimes the features that drift meaningfully aren't the ones we think to track - consider second-order effects, feature interactions, or implicit assumptions in feature engineering. Also: implicit feedback loops - if your model influences what users see, and what users see influences your training data, you can get feedback loops that look stable in features but create echo chambers. What's your exploration/exploitation balance, and are you logging counterfactual data?

### Human: We do have logged bandit data but mostly exploit mode - exploration is maybe 5%. You're right that we might be in a feedback loop. How do we diagnose that?

### Assistant: With 5% exploration, you're almost certainly in feedback loop territory. Diagnosis approaches: compare model predictions for exploration traffic versus exploitation traffic - systematic divergence indicates the model is fitting to its own previous predictions. Analyze long-term user engagement trends, not just immediate clicks - feedback loops often optimize for short-term engagement while degrading long-term satisfaction. Try counterfactual evaluation using inverse propensity scoring on your logged exploration data - this estimates how alternative recommendations would have performed. If off-policy evaluation shows significantly different top items than your current model produces, you've confirmed the loop.

### Human: If we confirm feedback loops, what's the intervention? More exploration is costly in user experience.

### Assistant: Several interventions beyond raw exploration: diversification injection at serving time (force some diversity without model changes), pessimistic training that down-weights predictions on items the model has already heavily promoted, or incorporating long-term engagement signals rather than just immediate interactions. Consider training on exploration data only or reweighting training to give exploration interactions higher influence. Some teams implement "interest discovery" flows that optimize for different objectives than the main feed. The harder question: your offline evaluation methodology needs updating. Logged bandit data with proper counterfactual evaluation is more reliable than naive holdout for systems with feedback loops. This is ongoing infrastructure investment, not a one-time fix.

### Human: This is helpful framing. What's the right level of exploration to maintain model health long-term?

### Assistant: There's no universal answer - it depends on action space size, distribution shift rate, and user tolerance. Empirically, many recommendation systems need 10-20% exploration to maintain adequate coverage for model learning. But "exploration" can be implemented with varying user cost: epsilon-greedy is expensive per-interaction, Thompson sampling or UCB approaches concentrate exploration on uncertain items rather than random ones, and contextual bandits can personalize exploration to users more tolerant of it. Consider: dedicated exploration cohorts, new user exploration phases, or exploring in lower-stakes surfaces. The cost-benefit shifts when you frame it as "investment in model health" rather than "degraded user experience."
