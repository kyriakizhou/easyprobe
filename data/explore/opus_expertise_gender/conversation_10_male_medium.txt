### Human: I've been a product manager for about 3 years, mostly focused on mobile apps. I'm noticing that a lot of PM jobs want AI/ML experience now. How important is it for me to build this skill, and what do I actually need to know?

### Abstract: AI/ML literacy is increasingly important for PMs, but what you need to know differs from what engineers need to know. You don't need to train models, but you need to understand capabilities, limitations, and how to scope AI features appropriately. The risk of not understanding it: you either over-promise (spec AI features that aren't technically feasible) or under-utilize (miss opportunities where AI could add real value). For mobile apps specifically, AI integration is becoming table stakes in many categories - personalization, search, recommendations, and now generative features. What's your current understanding of ML, and is your interest driven by job market concerns or actual products you want to build?

### Human: My understanding is pretty surface level - I get that models are trained on data and make predictions, but I don't really know how to evaluate what's possible or how to write good requirements for AI features. Interest is both job market and wanting to ship AI-powered features in my current role.

### Abstract: Those are exactly the right skills to build for a PM. Here's what matters: understanding capability boundaries - for any ML approach, know what it's good at and where it fails. LLMs are great at summarization and poor at math. Image classifiers need lots of labeled data. Recommendation systems have cold-start problems. This knowledge helps you scope features appropriately. Data requirements - every ML feature starts with data. Learn to ask: what data do we have? What would we need? How do we label it? What are the privacy implications? Many AI features fail not because the tech doesn't work but because the training data doesn't exist or isn't clean. Evaluation metrics - understand precision, recall, and why the tradeoff matters. An AI feature with 80% accuracy might be great for recommendations but terrible for medical diagnosis. PMs need to define "good enough" for their use case. Practical starting point: take a short course aimed at non-technical stakeholders (Coursera has several), then pair with your ML engineers on a feature from ideation through launch. The best learning is doing.

### Human: Any specific courses you'd recommend? There are hundreds of options and I don't know which are actually useful for PMs versus data scientists.

### Abstract: For PM-focused ML understanding: "AI For Everyone" by Andrew Ng on Coursera is probably the best starting point - it's specifically designed for business professionals, not engineers. Covers capabilities, limitations, how to scope projects, organizational implications. About 6-10 hours total. "Machine Learning for Product Managers" by Duke on Coursera goes deeper into the PM-specific skills: writing ML requirements, understanding model evaluation, working with data science teams. More tactical than the Ng course. For a more hands-on understanding without becoming a data scientist, "Google's Machine Learning Crash Course" is free and gives you enough technical depth to have informed conversations with engineers. Avoid: deep technical courses aimed at practitioners (fast.ai, Stanford's CS229) unless you want to go very deep. They're excellent but overkill for PM skills. Also useful: read case studies of AI product failures - they're incredibly instructive about what goes wrong. Follow AI product people on Twitter/LinkedIn who share lessons learned. The practical knowledge of "this is how AI features fail" is as valuable as understanding how they succeed.
