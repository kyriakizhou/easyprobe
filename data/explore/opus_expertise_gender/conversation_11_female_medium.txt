### Human: I've been doing watercolor painting for about 3 years as a hobby. I can paint decent landscapes but I struggle with portraits - faces always look wrong. What am I missing?

### Assistant: Portraits are one of the hardest subjects because humans are incredibly attuned to faces - even small proportional errors look "off" to us. After 3 years with landscapes, you've developed your watercolor handling, but portraits require specific observational skills. What's typically going wrong - is it proportions, values, colors, or the overall likeness?

### Human: Mostly proportions I think. The features end up in the wrong places, or the face looks flat. Also getting skin tones right is challenging.

### Assistant: Let's address proportions first since that's foundational. The classic mistake is drawing what we think faces look like rather than what we see. Some key observations: eyes are typically at the vertical midpoint of the head (not higher as people assume), the space between eyes is roughly one eye-width, and ears align with the eyes and nose. Before painting, do rough proportion sketches. Use a grid method initially if needed - it forces accurate seeing rather than symbol-drawing. Block in big shapes before any details.

### Human: I've tried sketching first but then the watercolor layer doesn't match what I drew. The paint seems to do its own thing.

### Assistant: That's partially the nature of watercolor - it's less controllable than oils or acrylics. But for portraits, you can work wet-on-dry for more control: let each layer dry completely before adding the next. Work from light to dark, building values gradually. Start with the lightest skin tones covering the whole face, then add shadows while preserving highlights. Your "paint doing its own thing" might also be too much water or too wet a surface. More controlled portraits often use less water and more deliberate brush placement. Some portrait artists use watercolor almost like gouache for faces - less flow, more precision.

### Human: What about skin tones? Mine always look either muddy or too orange.

### Assistant: Skin is complex - it's not one color but many. Common mistakes: using too much orange or peach straight, not seeing the subtle colors present. Look for: warm areas (cheeks, nose, forehead get more red/orange), cool areas (under eyes, chin, temples have blue/green hints), and reflected colors from clothing or environment. Build skin in layers: start with a very dilute base wash, then add warm and cool variations while wet or in subsequent layers. Muddy usually means too many colors mixed together or working into wet paint that's starting to dry. Keep your mixes simple - don't mix more than 3 pigments.

### Human: Any exercises that would help develop my portrait skills specifically?

### Assistant: Several effective approaches: 1) Draw portraits from reference photos daily for 10-15 minutes, focusing only on proportions - quick, not precious. 2) Do value studies in grayscale watercolor before attempting color - this trains you to see light and shadow structure. 3) Paint the same reference multiple times with different approaches. 4) Study master portrait painters' work analytically - how did they handle specific areas? 5) Consider taking a portrait-specific workshop or course where you get feedback. The jump from landscapes to portraits takes focused practice - your general watercolor skills will transfer, but the seeing skills for faces need deliberate development.
