### Human: I'm a managing director in restructuring at an investment bank. We're seeing increased activity in liability management transactions - uptiers, dropdown transactions, and other creditor-on-creditor violence. How do you think about advising clients in this environment where the rules seem to be changing?

### Assistant: The proliferation of aggressive liability management transactions has fundamentally changed restructuring practice. What used to be considered violations of intercreditor norms are now standard playbook moves, and advisors need to adapt while managing significant ethical and reputational considerations. The current landscape: the Serta, Boardriders, TriMark, and subsequent transactions established that sponsors and select creditor groups can engineer transactions that subordinate or extract value from non-participating lenders. Courts have generally permitted these transactions where they follow the letter of credit agreements, even when they violate traditional expectations of pro-rata treatment. Key transaction types: uptier exchanges: offering select creditors the opportunity to exchange into new super-priority debt, subordinating non-participants. Dropdown transactions: moving valuable assets to unrestricted subsidiaries outside the credit group, then raising new money against those assets. Extension and exchange with priming: extending maturities while offering priming positions to participants. Your advisory positioning question: are you typically advising the company/sponsor (offense), the participating creditor group (selective benefit), or the excluded/disadvantaged creditors (defense)? Each position has different considerations.

### Human: We work both sides, but increasingly we're being asked to advise ad hoc groups of creditors who are trying to protect themselves - either by participating in transactions or by blocking them. The challenge is navigating situations where different creditors within seemingly similar positions have divergent interests.

### Abstract: Advising ad hoc creditor groups in this environment is particularly complex because the transactions are often designed to fracture creditor solidarity. The advisor's role requires navigating these fracture points. Key dynamics you're managing: coalition fragility: ad hoc groups form around perceived common interest, but LME transactions specifically target fault lines within creditor coalitions. A creditor group that appears unified in opposing a transaction may fragment when some members receive attractive participation terms. Your job includes assessing and managing this fragmentation risk. Information asymmetry: sponsors and company advisors often approach subset of creditors with transaction terms before others know. By the time an ad hoc group forms, some potential members may already be in discussions or have signed NDAs. You're often working with incomplete information about who's already been co-opted. Blocking versus participating calculus: the strategic question for each creditor: is the expected recovery higher from blocking the transaction (if possible) or from participating? This depends on legal blocking positions (covenant protections, voting thresholds), practical blocking ability (can you actually prevent it or just make it harder?), participation terms available, and likely outcome if blocked versus consummated. Different creditors within a group may have different answers based on their basis, duration tolerance, and risk appetite. Fiduciary considerations: you're advising a group, but individual members have their own fiduciary duties and economic interests. You can't prevent members from pursuing individual interests, including potentially defecting to participate. Managing client expectations about group cohesion is essential. Practical advisory approach: early legal analysis of what the documents actually permit - many transactions hinge on specific covenant language. Identify potential blocking positions before they're needed. Facilitate creditor communication while managing antitrust and information-sharing concerns. Help creditors understand their individual optionality within the group context.

### Human: On the legal analysis point - we're finding that even sophisticated creditors are surprised by what their documents permit. How should we be thinking about credit agreement diligence going forward, and how do you counsel clients who are considering new investments?

### Abstract: Credit agreement diligence has become a specialized discipline that many investors underappreciated until they got burned by LME transactions. The "standard" terms that seemed adequate in a cooperative restructuring environment have proven to have significant exploitable gaps. Diligence framework for new investments: J. Crew and Serta protections: after the landmark cases, the market developed specific "J. Crew blockers" and "Serta protections." Check whether the credit agreement includes: limitations on moving material IP or assets to unrestricted subsidiaries (J. Crew protection), requirements for pro-rata treatment in open market purchases and debt exchanges, limitations on incurrence of priming debt without broader consent, restrictions on designating material subsidiaries as unrestricted. Many pre-2020 vintages lack these protections; newer deals vary significantly. "Sacred rights" analysis: identify which terms require unanimous or supermajority consent versus simple majority. Historically, lenders assumed collective action would prevent value extraction even where documents permitted it. Now you need to assume that anything permitted by documents may be attempted. Specific provisions to scrutinize: unrestricted subsidiary baskets and designation capacity, incremental debt capacity (how much, what priority, what conditions), collateral release provisions, guarantee release provisions, amendment and waiver thresholds for different terms, definition of "open market purchases" and debt exchange provisions. Counsel clients on basis-adjusted protection: investors buying at discount have different risk profiles than par lenders. A transaction that violates traditional norms but allows full recovery at discount basis may be acceptable. Diligence should inform not just whether to invest but at what price protection gaps are adequately compensated. Going forward recommendations: treat credit agreement analysis as primary investment consideration, not secondary legal review. Maintain database of document terms across credits for pattern recognition. Engage specialized liability management counsel early, not just when problems emerge. Price credits with LME-vulnerable terms at appropriate discount.
