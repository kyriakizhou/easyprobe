### Human: I've been doing event planning as a side business for about 4 years - mostly birthday parties, showers, and small corporate events. I'm considering making it full-time but worried about the inconsistency of income. How do I evaluate this?

### Assistant: Going full-time with an event business is a significant decision! The income inconsistency concern is valid - events are seasonal and lumpy. Let me understand your current situation: what's your average annual revenue from events, what's your profit margin after expenses, and what are your personal financial obligations?

### Human: Revenue is about $40K per year, maybe 60% profit after expenses. I'd need around $50K to replace my current salary. I have some savings but not a huge cushion.

### Assistant: So you're netting around $24K currently and would need to nearly triple that for full salary replacement. That's a significant gap. Options to consider: grow the business to target revenue before quitting (prove the market exists), transition gradually by reducing day job hours if possible, or set a clear financial milestone (X months of savings, Y revenue run rate) before jumping. What's limiting your growth now - time, marketing, market size, or pricing?

### Human: Mostly time. I can only take so many events while working full-time. I've turned down business because I couldn't handle it. I also price low because I'm not full-time.

### Assistant: Turning down business is actually encouraging data! It suggests demand exists that you can't currently serve. Pricing being low "because not full-time" is a mindset trap - your work has value regardless of your employment status. Before going full-time: raise your prices now (your limited availability justifies premium pricing), track what you're turning down to estimate true demand, and build a booking buffer (have several months of future events booked before you leave). If you can raise prices 30-40% and you're already turning down work, the math might work faster than you think.

### Human: What about the seasonal nature? Corporate events are fall, weddings are summer, and January-February is dead.

### Assistant: Seasonality is real and requires planning. Strategies: diversify event types to smooth the calendar (what happens in winter? holiday parties help, but add things like milestone birthdays, intimate gatherings), build corporate retainer relationships for recurring events, offer planning services in slow seasons for events happening later, create passive income streams (planning templates, courses, vendor referral commissions), and budget on annual basis rather than monthly - save summer/fall income for winter. Many event planners supplement with related work: day-of coordination, vendor styling, or consulting. Knowing your slow season lets you plan: that's when you do marketing, system building, and take time off.

### Human: How do I test whether I can actually sustain this before fully committing?

### Assistant: A phased test: First, raise prices immediately - if demand holds, that's positive signal. Second, use vacation or reduced hours to take on more events for a quarter and see how it feels and performs. Third, set specific go/no-go criteria: "If I book X events at Y price over the next 6 months, I'll transition." Fourth, build runway - enough savings to cover 6-12 months of expenses gives you room to build without panic. Fifth, have a contingency plan - what's your path back to employment if it doesn't work? Testing shows you actual data rather than projections. Many successful full-time business owners spent 1-2 years in intentional "testing" phase.
