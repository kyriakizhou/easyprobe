### Human: I'm the chief investment officer at a family office managing about $500M. We're evaluating our allocation to private equity and debating between fund commitments versus co-investments versus direct deals. What's your framework for thinking through this?

### Assistant: The fund versus co-invest versus direct spectrum involves tradeoffs across diversification, control, costs, access, and operational requirements. Each has a role depending on your capabilities and objectives. Framework by approach: fund commitments: maximum diversification across deals, managers, and vintages. Delegate sourcing and execution to professional managers. Highest fee burden (typically 1.5-2% management fee plus 20% carry). Least control over specific investments. Minimum operational burden on your team. Access to managers you couldn't replicate independently. Co-investments: invest alongside GPs in specific deals, usually with reduced or no fees. Requires ability to evaluate deals with limited time and information. Provides concentrated exposure to specific opportunities you like. Maintains alignment with professional sponsor. Requires relationship access - GPs offer co-invest to their best LPs. Direct investments: complete control and no fees, but maximum operational burden. Requires full sourcing, diligence, execution, and portfolio company oversight capabilities. Concentrated risk without portfolio diversification. Most family offices that attempt this underestimate the resources required. Key question for your situation: what are your actual operational capabilities and investment team resources? At $500M total AUM, how much of that is allocated to PE, and what does your team look like for private market investing?

### Human: PE allocation is currently about $100M across commitments to 5 funds. We have a 3-person investment team including myself. We're getting offered co-investment opportunities from our GP relationships and trying to decide whether to pursue them. I'm also being approached by independent sponsors and fundless deals that would be more direct.

### Abstract: With $100M in PE, 5 fund relationships, and a 3-person team, you're at an interesting inflection point. Co-investments from existing GPs are likely attractive; true direct deals require more caution. Co-investment assessment: your GP relationships should be generating some co-invest flow - this is one of the benefits of being an LP. Considerations for your situation: selectivity is critical: you'll be offered co-invests, but you should only accept a fraction. GPs sometimes offer co-invest on deals where they want to reduce their own concentration or where deal economics require LP capital. Evaluate each opportunity on its own merits, not just because it's offered. Your diligence capacity is limited with a 3-person team, so be highly selective. Time pressure: co-invests often have compressed timelines - the GP has already done their work and needs capital commitment in weeks, not months. Assess whether you can realistically diligence opportunities in the timeframe offered. If you can't, it's better to pass than to invest based on trust alone. Sector/strategy focus: rather than evaluating every co-invest opportunistically, focus on sectors where your team has genuine expertise. Saying "we only look at healthcare services co-invests" allows you to build pattern recognition and diligence faster. Concentration risk: a few co-invests can significantly concentrate your PE portfolio. If your fund commitments provide diversification across 50-100 companies and you add 3-4 co-invests, those co-invests might be 20-30% of your PE exposure. Size positions accordingly. Independent sponsors and fundless deals: this is a different risk category. Without GP infrastructure, you're taking on more diligence burden, monitoring responsibility, and operator quality risk. At your team size, I'd be cautious about true direct deals unless you have specific domain expertise or can leverage operating partners. The fee savings don't compensate for the additional work and risk in most cases.

### Human: On co-investments specifically - how do we actually build the capability to diligence these opportunities quickly enough to meet GP timelines while our team has other responsibilities?

### Abstract: Building co-investment diligence capability with a small team requires systematization and smart use of external resources. You can't replicate a GP's deal team, but you can develop efficient processes for the subset of opportunities you pursue. Practical capability building: pre-built diligence frameworks by sector: for sectors you'll focus on (hopefully 2-3 max), develop standardized diligence frameworks in advance. Key financial metrics to evaluate, typical deal structures, valuation benchmarks, common risks, standard quality of earnings issues. When an opportunity arrives, you're not starting from scratch - you're populating a template you've already built. Leverage GP materials efficiently: the GP has already done extensive diligence. Request their full investment memo, QoE report, and management presentation. Your job isn't to replicate their work - it's to pressure-test key assumptions and identify risks they might be underweighting. Focus on 3-5 critical issues per deal, not comprehensive diligence. External diligence support: build relationships with advisors who can support rapid diligence. An accounting firm that can review QoE findings on short notice, an industry expert network for market checks, a legal advisor familiar with co-invest structures. These relationships should be established before you need them. Expert network calls: services like GLG or AlphaSights can arrange expert calls within days. Two or three expert calls on an industry or specific company can validate or challenge key thesis elements quickly. Staged decision process: rather than trying to complete full diligence before any commitment, consider a staged approach - verbal indication of interest based on initial review, then deeper diligence before final documentation. This buys time while signaling serious intent to the GP. Team role clarity: with 3 people, assign clear roles when an opportunity comes in. One person owns the investment thesis and market analysis, one owns financial/valuation analysis, one coordinates experts and external support. Parallel processing is essential given timelines. Capacity management: be honest about your capacity. If you're already evaluating one co-invest, you probably can't properly evaluate a second simultaneously. Build relationship capital by passing clearly and quickly on opportunities you can't evaluate properly rather than slow-walking and wasting GP time.
