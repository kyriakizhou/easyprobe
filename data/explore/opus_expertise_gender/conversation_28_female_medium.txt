### Human: I've been playing acoustic guitar for about 5 years and I'm comfortable with fingerpicking and common chord shapes. I want to explore jazz guitar but the theory seems overwhelming. Where should I start?

### Assistant: Jazz guitar can feel like learning a new language, but you have a solid foundation to build on! The theory seems overwhelming because jazz uses more complex harmony than folk or pop. The good news: you don't need to learn everything at once. Start with understanding how jazz harmony differs: extended chords (7ths, 9ths, 13ths), altered chords, and functional harmony (how chords relate to each other). What draws you to jazz - the chord voicings, improvisation, or specific players?

### Human: I love the sophisticated chord voicings I hear in jazz standards. My folk background means I'm mostly playing open chords and barre chords. The jazz chords look completely different.

### Assistant: You've identified the first big shift! Jazz guitarists use different voicing concepts. Key principles: shell voicings (root, 3rd, 7th - minimal but essential tones), drop 2 and drop 3 voicings (different note inversions across strings), and voice leading (moving smoothly between chords with minimal hand movement). Start by learning just the basic maj7, min7, and dominant 7 chord shapes on the middle four strings - these are foundational. Unlike folk where you might play full six-string chords, jazz voicings are often 3-4 notes. Less is more; each note has a function.

### Human: What about those Roman numeral progressions I see? I understand basic I-IV-V but jazz seems way more complex.

### Assistant: Jazz harmony uses more chromatic movement and chord substitutions, but it's still rooted in functional harmony. The most common jazz progression is ii-V-I (for example, Dm7 - G7 - Cmaj7 in the key of C). Learn this progression in several keys - it's everywhere. Once you can play and hear ii-V-I, you'll recognize it in countless standards. From there, learn common variations: minor ii-V-i, the I-vi-ii-V turnaround, and tritone substitutions. Don't try to learn all substitutions at once. Get the basic progressions in your ears and fingers first. A tool like iReal Pro can help you practice common changes.

### Human: Should I be learning to read music? I've always used tabs and chord charts.

### Assistant: For jazz, reading standard notation becomes more valuable than in other genres. Lead sheets (melody with chord symbols) are how jazz musicians communicate repertoire. You don't need to sight-read perfectly, but being able to decode a lead sheet - reading the melody and understanding the chord symbols - opens up the jazz standard repertoire. Start by learning to read rhythms and notes in first position, then gradually extend. Many jazz guitarists read competently but aren't virtuoso readers. Also learn to interpret chord symbols fluently (Cmaj7#11, G7alt, etc.) - that's where your harmonic decisions come from.

### Human: Any recommended paths or resources for making this transition?

### Assistant: A few approaches: Jody Fisher's "Jazz Guitar" series is systematic and builds progressively. "The Jazz Theory Book" by Mark Levine is the theory bible (not guitar-specific but essential concepts). For guitarists specifically, Ted Greene's work on chord voicings is legendary. Get a Real Book (the standard jazz fakebook) and start learning standards - "Autumn Leaves," "All The Things You Are," and "Blue Bossa" are common starters. YouTube channels like Jens Larsen offer free jazz guitar content. Consider lessons with a jazz guitarist, at least for a few months - having someone diagnose what YOU specifically need to work on accelerates learning dramatically. And listen constantly - let your ear lead your fingers.
