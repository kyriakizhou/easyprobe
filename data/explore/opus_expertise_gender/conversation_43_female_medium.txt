### Human: I've been doing stand-up comedy for about 3 years at open mics and small showcases. I want to start getting paid work but I don't know how that transition happens. How do comedians go from free shows to paid gigs?

### Assistant: The open mic to paid work transition is one of the haziest parts of comedy! The path varies, but generally involves: building a tight set that consistently works, getting noticed by bookers and other comedians, and actively pursuing opportunities. After 3 years, you likely have 15-20 minutes of tested material. How would you describe your current set strength, and are you in a market with a comedy scene?

### Human: I'm in a mid-size city with a few comedy clubs and bars that do shows. My 10-minute set is pretty solid - usually gets good responses. I haven't pushed for longer sets much.

### Assistant: Building to 20-30 minutes is important - that's headliner territory at smaller venues and opener slots at clubs. But your 10 minutes can start generating paid work now. Entry-level paid gigs: hosting/MCing comedy nights (often $25-75 plus the experience), corporate events or private parties (can pay well but require clean material), bars and restaurants starting comedy nights (they need reliable local acts), and opening for touring comedians at local clubs. The key is visibility to bookers. Are you performing regularly at the clubs that do paid shows?

### Human: I go to their open mics but haven't really talked to the bookers or staff much. Should I be networking more aggressively?

### Assistant: Networking is essential, but "aggressive" isn't quite the right frame. Think of it as building relationships over time. Show up consistently, be professional and easy to work with, don't burn bridges with other comics (comedy scenes are small), and make yourself useful - help with shows, bring people to your sets. Bookers notice reliability before talent. When you do approach bookers: be respectful of their time, have a video of your best set ready to share, ask if they have hosting needs or opening slots rather than demanding headline spots. Most bookers prefer booking people they know and have seen perform multiple times.

### Human: What about submitting to festivals? I've heard that's important for advancement.

### Assistant: Festivals can be valuable for exposure, networking, and credibility. But be strategic - there are hundreds of comedy festivals and most don't directly lead to career advancement. The ones that matter most: Edinburgh Fringe (significant investment, potentially career-making), JFL Montreal (industry-focused, invitation/submission), and regional festivals that attract industry scouts. Smaller festivals build credits and network. When submitting: have a strong video (clean audio, good crowd response), follow submission guidelines exactly, apply broadly since acceptance is unpredictable, and budget for expenses (most festivals don't pay travel). At 3 years, building regional credits makes sense; major festivals can come later.

### Human: How do I know if I'm ready to pursue this more seriously versus keeping it as a hobby?

### Assistant: Honest self-assessment: Do you consistently get laughs from audiences who don't know you (not just supportive friends)? Can you write new material regularly? Do you want to do this badly enough to endure years of low pay and rejection? Talk to working comics in your scene honestly about the reality. Test your readiness: start pursuing paid gigs and see what happens - rejection is data. You can pursue it "seriously" while maintaining day job income; most working comedians do for years. The decision isn't hobby versus career as a binary - it's a spectrum you move along as opportunities and commitment allow.
