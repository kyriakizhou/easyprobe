### Human: I've been doing landscape photography for a few years now and I've got a decent portfolio. I'm starting to think about selling prints but I have no idea how to price them or where to sell them.

### Assistant: The photography print market is challenging but viable if you approach it strategically. A few questions to frame this: what's your goal - supplementary income, covering hobby costs, or building toward making photography your primary income? Are you thinking online sales, local galleries, art fairs, or all of the above? And what print sizes and mediums have you been making for your own portfolio? Understanding your current capabilities and goals helps determine the right approach.

### Human: Main goal is to cover some of my gear costs and see if there's real demand before I go further. I've mostly printed smaller sizes (8x12, 11x14) for myself but I have high-res files that could go larger. I'd prefer online initially since art fairs seem like a lot of work for uncertain return.

### Abstract: Starting online to test demand is smart before investing in art fair inventory. For pricing, there are several schools of thought: cost-plus (print cost + framing + your time + profit margin), market comparison (what similar photographers charge), or value-based (what your audience will pay for the emotional/aesthetic value). For starting out, I'd suggest: research photographers at your level (not the famous ones) and see their pricing. For smaller prints (8x12, 11x14), $50-150 unframed is typical for emerging photographers; larger prints (16x24, 20x30) run $150-350. Don't price too low - it signals amateur work and makes it hard to raise prices later. For platforms: your own website with Squarespace or Shopify gives you control and better margins but requires driving your own traffic. Etsy has built-in traffic but high competition and fees. Fine art marketplaces like Saatchi Art or Artfinder are curated and credible but take significant commissions (30-40%). What's your current social media or online presence like?

### Human: I have an Instagram with about 3,000 followers but I've never tried to sell through it. Mostly just sharing my work and connecting with other photographers.

### Abstract: 3,000 engaged followers is actually a decent foundation for testing sales - many photographers sell primarily through Instagram, especially for prints under $500. Here's an approach: set up a simple print store on your own site (Squarespace or a dedicated service like Pixieset or SmugMug which are built for photographers). Then start occasionally mentioning that prints are available - not hard-selling, but "this one is available as a print" or posting a print in a frame in a home setting. Many photographers do a limited edition release of specific images which creates urgency and makes followers feel like they're getting something special. Run a small test: pick 3-5 of your strongest images, make them available, price them reasonably (maybe $75-150 for smaller sizes), and announce it to your audience. See what happens. This costs you almost nothing, gives you real market feedback, and you can adjust from there. Don't invest in inventory upfront - use print-on-demand initially so you're only printing what sells.
