### Human: I'm a principal research scientist leading a team working on protein structure prediction at a pharmaceutical company. With AlphaFold having solved much of the structure prediction problem, I'm trying to think about where the field goes next and what problems become tractable. What's your perspective?

### Assistant: AlphaFold's success has fundamentally changed the landscape of computational structural biology. The shift from "structure prediction is unsolved" to "structure prediction is largely solved for monomeric proteins" has opened new problem spaces while making some previous research directions less valuable. Reassessing the post-AlphaFold landscape: what AlphaFold solved: single-chain static structure prediction is largely solved for proteins with homologs in training data. Accuracy for many targets approaches experimental resolution. This is transformative for the field. What AlphaFold doesn't fully solve: protein-protein and protein-ligand complexes (AlphaFold-Multimer helps but significant gaps remain), dynamics and conformational ensembles (AlphaFold predicts single structures, not the ensemble of states proteins actually sample), intrinsically disordered proteins and regions, effects of mutations on structure and stability, structure in cellular context (membranes, crowding, post-translational modifications), and proteins with no evolutionary homologs (de novo designed proteins, novel folds). New tractable problems: with structural bottleneck removed, problems that were blocked by lack of structures become tractable. Protein function prediction at scale: we now have structures for essentially all known proteins. Function annotation using structural analysis becomes feasible. Large-scale binding site analysis: systematic analysis of potential binding sites across proteomes. Interaction network structural analysis: moving from sequence-based interaction networks to structure-informed networks. What's driving your team's strategic direction - fundamental research or drug discovery applications?

### Human: We're focused on drug discovery applications. Our interest is specifically in understanding how to use structural information to better predict binding affinity and design molecules that will bind to targets. AlphaFold gives us structures, but we still struggle with the affinity prediction and molecular design problems.

### Abstract: Binding affinity prediction and structure-based molecular design are indeed the bottleneck problems now that structure prediction is largely solved. These problems are hard for fundamental reasons, but there's significant progress and opportunity. The binding affinity challenge: why it's hard: binding affinity depends on subtle energy differences (enthalpy, entropy, solvation) that are difficult to compute accurately. The binding site is flexible, the ligand is flexible, water networks reorganize, and the relevant energy differences are often 1-2 kcal/mol (comparable to thermal noise). Free energy calculations have improved but remain computationally expensive and often insufficiently accurate for rank-ordering compounds. Physics-based versus ML approaches: traditional physics-based methods (FEP, TI) are rigorous but expensive and require careful setup. ML methods (GNN-based affinity predictors, 3D CNNs on binding sites) are fast but struggle with generalization - they often capture dataset biases rather than true binding physics. Hybrid approaches incorporating physical constraints into ML models are a promising direction. Data limitations: high-quality binding affinity data is limited. PDBbind and similar resources provide thousands of complexes with affinity data, but this is small by ML standards. Data augmentation, transfer learning from related tasks, and active learning approaches address this partially. Actionable research directions: protein-ligand co-folding: predicting bound complex structures given only sequences and SMILES, rather than requiring apo structures. AlphaFold-derived approaches adapted for ligand binding show promise but aren't yet reliable. ML-integrated free energy methods: using ML to accelerate or guide FEP calculations - identifying promising transformations, improving sampling, or learning corrections to physics-based energies. Generative design with affinity optimization: generative models (diffusion, autoregressive) for molecule generation conditioned on binding sites and affinity objectives. The challenge is ensuring generated molecules are synthesizable and satisfy other drug-like properties. The structure-to-function gap in general: AlphaFold gives you structure, but understanding how structure translates to binding and function remains the frontier.

### Human: On the generative design front - we've experimented with diffusion models for molecule generation but struggle with multi-objective optimization. The molecules optimized for predicted binding often fail on other properties like synthesizability or ADMET. How do you approach this?

### Abstract: Multi-objective optimization in generative molecular design is one of the core challenges, and the failure mode you describe - optimizing one objective at the expense of others - is universal. The generated molecules exploit weaknesses in surrogate models rather than representing true drug candidates. Approaches to multi-objective molecular generation: objective architecture: Pareto optimization: rather than collapsing to single objective, maintain Pareto frontier of non-dominated solutions across objectives. Let medicinal chemists choose from frontier based on project priorities. This is more honest about tradeoffs than weighted sums. Constrained optimization: treat some objectives (synthesizability, molecular weight, PAINS filters) as hard constraints rather than soft objectives. Only explore chemical space that satisfies constraints. This prevents the model from finding loopholes in soft penalties. Hierarchical generation: generate scaffolds satisfying basic drug-likeness constraints first, then elaborate with binding-focused optimization. This embeds hard constraints architecturally rather than through training objectives. Improved surrogate models: better synthesizability prediction: synthetic accessibility scores (SA Score, SCScore, retrosynthesis-based) are imperfect. Invest in better synthesizability prediction or integrate with actual retrosynthesis planning tools that provide synthesis routes, not just scores. ADMET model uncertainty: ADMET models have significant uncertainty. Use ensemble methods to quantify uncertainty and penalize or flag molecules where ADMET predictions are unreliable. Avoid optimizing molecules that exploit model weaknesses by staying in distribution. Adversarial robustness: your surrogate models are vulnerable to adversarial examples - molecules that score well but are actually poor binders or have poor properties. Train or filter for robustness against these failure modes. Process and validation: early synthesis and testing: close the loop between generation and validation early. Don't generate thousands of molecules and then synthesize - generate smaller batches, synthesize representative molecules, and use experimental feedback to improve generation. Active learning cycles: iteratively refine generative models and surrogates based on experimental results. The initial model may generate exploitable molecules, but feedback corrects this over time. Chemist-in-the-loop: don't fully automate. Have medicinal chemists review generated molecules for plausibility before synthesis. Their intuition catches failure modes that metrics miss.
