### Human: I inherited some money and everyone keeps telling me different things about what to do with it. I'm completely lost.

### Abstract: Inheriting money can be overwhelming, especially when you're getting conflicting advice. First, take a breath - there's no rush to do anything immediately. The money isn't going anywhere while you figure this out. Don't let anyone pressure you into decisions. Can you share roughly how much we're talking about and your current financial situation - do you have debts, an emergency fund, retirement savings? Also, are people trying to give you generic advice, or are they actively trying to get you to invest with them? The latter is a red flag.

### Human: It's about $50,000. I have a little bit of credit card debt, a small emergency fund, and I do have a 401k but not much in it. Mostly family giving opinions, no one is trying to sell me anything.

### Abstract: $50,000 is a meaningful amount that can really set you up well. Here's a clear priority order that most financial advisors would agree on: first, pay off that credit card debt completely. Credit cards charge 20%+ interest - no investment consistently beats that. Paying it off is an instant guaranteed return. Second, build your emergency fund to 3-6 months of expenses. This protects you from going back into debt if something unexpected happens. Third, consider maxing out your 401k contributions for a year or two using the inheritance to supplement your income, especially if you get an employer match. Fourth, whatever's left could go into a Roth IRA (up to $7,000/year) or a regular brokerage account in low-cost index funds. The boring answer is usually the right one: eliminate debt, build security, then invest for the future. Don't feel pressure to do anything flashy.

### Human: People are suggesting I buy property or invest in individual stocks. Those seem more exciting than just index funds.

### Abstract: I get it - index funds sound boring compared to picking winning stocks or owning real estate. But here's the thing: "exciting" in investing usually means "risky" and historically, boring wins. The data is clear: over time, most individual stock pickers and even most professional fund managers underperform simple index funds. Real estate can be a good investment, but $50,000 isn't enough for a down payment in most markets, and being a landlord is a part-time job with its own risks and headaches. If you put $50,000 in an index fund and add to it over time, you could have several hundred thousand dollars in 20-30 years without any clever moves. With individual stocks, you might do great or you might lose half of it. With $50,000, you're at a stage where protecting and steadily growing this money matters more than swinging for the fences. The foundation (emergency fund, no high-interest debt, retirement contributions) is the unsexy work that actually builds wealth. Once you have that locked in, you could take small amounts for "fun" investments if you want.
