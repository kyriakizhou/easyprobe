### Human: I want to start composting but I live in an apartment with no yard. Is it even possible?

### Abstract: Absolutely possible! Apartment composting is more common than you'd think. You have a few options: a small countertop compost bin with a charcoal filter (controls smell) that you empty weekly at a community compost drop-off, a bokashi bin system (ferments food waste in a sealed container, very compact and odor-free), or even a small worm bin (vermicomposting) that breaks down food scraps indoors. The right choice depends on: whether your city has compost collection or drop-off points, how much food waste you generate, and your comfort level with different methods. What's your food waste situation like, and does your area have any compost pickup or drop-off?

### Human: I cook pretty regularly so I have a decent amount of scraps. I think there's a farmers market nearby that takes compost. The worm thing sounds kind of gross though.

### Abstract: Totally understandable on the worms - it's not for everyone! If there's a farmers market drop-off, you're in great shape. Here's the simple approach: get a countertop compost bin with a lid and charcoal filter (about $25-40, look for brands like OXO or Bamboozle). Keep it on your counter or under the sink. Throw in fruit and vegetable scraps, coffee grounds, tea bags, eggshells. Avoid meat, dairy, and oily foods - they smell bad and most drop-off locations don't accept them. When it's full (probably weekly), take the bag to the farmers market drop-off. That's really it! Use compostable bags as liners to make transport cleaner. If smell is ever an issue, empty more frequently or keep it in the freezer until drop-off day. It becomes routine really quickly once you start.

### Human: What can and can't go in? I'd hate to mess it up.

### Abstract: It's simpler than you think! Generally yes: fruit and vegetable scraps (peels, cores, rinds), coffee grounds and filters, tea bags (remove staples), eggshells, bread and grains, plain pasta or rice, nut shells, shredded paper and cardboard, paper towels and napkins (unbleached). Generally no: meat, fish, and bones (smell and attract pests), dairy products, oily or greasy foods, pet waste, diseased plants. The drop-off location might have specific rules, so check their signage when you first go. But fruits, veggies, and coffee grounds are universally accepted and probably make up most kitchen scraps anyway. If you're ever unsure about something, just throw it in regular trash - it's better to leave something out than contaminate a batch. Start simple with the obvious stuff (banana peels, apple cores, coffee grounds) and you'll quickly get a feel for it.
