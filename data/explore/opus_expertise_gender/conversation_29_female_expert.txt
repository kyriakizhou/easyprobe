### Human: I'm restructuring the compensation architecture for a Series D fintech startup preparing for IPO. Current equity structure is complicated by multiple rounds at different valuations and significant early employee grants that are deep underwater. How should we think about equity refresh and retention ahead of the public market transition?

### Assistant: Pre-IPO equity restructuring is complex when you have underwater grants and valuation history that creates uneven employee situations. Key dimensions: retention risk assessment (who has underwater equity and is critical to retain?), incentive alignment (does current equity structure motivate IPO execution?), dilution management (how much additional equity can you grant without excessive dilution?), and fairness perception (how will changes be received by different employee cohorts?). The underwater equity problem is common after multiple rounds - employees who joined at peak valuations may have grants with strike prices above current or expected IPO valuation. Options include repricing (complex tax implications), exchange programs (new grants in exchange for cancelled underwater grants), or supplemental grants (additional equity that provides upside even if original grants don't). What's your current cap table situation regarding employee pool, and how underwater are the problematic grants?

### Human: About 15% of fully diluted shares in employee pool, roughly half already allocated. Many grants from our Series C round have $45 strike prices but we're likely IPO-ing around $30-35 per share. Some of our most critical engineering talent is in this cohort.

### Assistant: $45 strikes with $30-35 IPO pricing means those grants are worthless at IPO and may never be in the money absent significant post-IPO appreciation. For critical talent, that's a retention emergency. Options for underwater grants: option repricing to current fair market value - creates taxable event under 409A and requires shareholder approval, accounting charge, but directly addresses the problem; option exchange program where employees can exchange underwater options for fewer options at lower strike price (value-neutral but more likelihood of value realization); or supplemental RSU grants that provide value regardless of historical strike prices - doesn't require touching existing grants but increases dilution. For your situation: the Series C cohort likely includes engineers hired during rapid scaling who are essential to IPO execution. Supplemental RSUs are often the cleanest approach - existing grants remain unchanged, new grants provide retention value and upside alignment. Size the RSUs to make total compensation competitive assuming existing options have zero value. What's your remaining pool capacity after current allocations?

### Human: About 7.5% remaining in the pool. Board is pushing back on additional pool increases given dilution concerns ahead of IPO.

### Assistant: 7.5% remaining pool with board dilution sensitivity is tight but workable if you prioritize ruthlessly. Framework: segment employees into tiers based on retention criticality and underwater exposure. Tier 1 (critical talent with significantly underwater equity) gets meaningful supplemental grants; Tier 2 (important contributors with some underwater exposure) gets smaller supplements; Tier 3 (less critical or less underwater) gets minimal or no additional grants. This is uncomfortable but necessary given pool constraints. For Tier 1 retention targets: model what compensation package they'd receive if recruited externally, and ensure your total compensation (salary, bonus, new RSUs, assuming underwater options are worthless) is competitive. You may need to accept that some of your pool goes to a relatively small number of critical people. Also consider: post-IPO, you'll establish a new equity refresh program with annual grants. The bridge you're building is to IPO; post-IPO compensation planning is a separate exercise with different constraints. Can you identify how many employees fall into each criticality tier?

### Human: We've identified about 40 people in the critical tier, representing our core technical leadership and key individual contributors. Getting them competitive packages probably requires 3-4% of fully diluted shares.

### Assistant: 3-4% for 40 critical people is about half your remaining pool, which is significant but defensible if retention of those 40 is genuinely essential to IPO execution. Board conversation: frame this as retention insurance with quantified risk - what's the IPO execution risk if you lose 20% of that critical cohort in the 12-18 months before IPO? What's the recruiting cost and timeline to replace senior technical talent? The dilution cost of retention is almost certainly lower than the execution risk and replacement cost of attrition. Structure for the grants: RSUs vesting over 3-4 years with meaningful vesting at IPO or shortly after (perhaps 25% at IPO, remainder over subsequent years) - this provides immediate retention value while maintaining long-term alignment. Consider double-trigger acceleration for change of control if you haven't already. For remaining pool after critical retention: preserve capacity for new hires during IPO execution period and for addressing any emerging retention issues. Don't fully allocate. Post-IPO refresh programs typically add 1-2% annual dilution; communicate to employees that the supplemental grants are a bridge to a regular refresh program. What's your IPO timeline and anticipated lock-up structure?

### Human: IPO probably 12-15 months out, standard 180-day lock-up. Some of our critical people are asking about secondary liquidity before IPO.

### Assistant: Pre-IPO secondary liquidity requests add complexity but are increasingly common and can be a retention tool. Options: company-facilitated tender offer allowing employees to sell a portion of vested equity to existing investors or new secondary buyers - this requires board approval and valuation determination, but provides liquidity without waiting for IPO. Alternatively, some companies allow individual secondary sales with ROFR waiver, though this is messier and creates price discovery issues. Considerations for your situation: secondary liquidity is most valuable for employees with low-strike grants who actually have in-the-money equity - your underwater Series C cohort doesn't benefit from secondary because they have nothing to sell. This cuts two ways: liquidity programs may not address your primary retention problem, but also don't cost you anything for the underwater population. For employees with meaningful vested equity (likely earlier joiners with lower strikes): limited secondary can be a retention tool that demonstrates good faith without diluting anyone. Structure carefully - typically cap participation at 10-20% of vested holdings to maintain alignment through IPO. What's your investor appetite for participating in secondary or facilitating a tender process?
