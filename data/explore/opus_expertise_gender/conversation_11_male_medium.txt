### Human: I've been grilling and smoking meat for a few years and I want to enter some local BBQ competitions. What should I know about competition BBQ before I jump in?

### Assistant: Competition BBQ is a different game from backyard cooking - the judging criteria, timing requirements, and presentation standards create a specific optimization problem. The main sanctioning bodies (KCBS, IBCA, MBN) have different rules and judging criteria, so you'll want to pick one to start. KCBS is the biggest and most common. The four meat categories (chicken, ribs, pork, brisket) are judged on appearance, taste, and tenderness with specific scoring rubrics. What's your strongest meat right now, and have you attended any competitions as a spectator to see how they run?

### Human: I'm best at ribs and pork shoulder. Haven't been to a competition yet but I've watched a lot of videos. I was thinking of starting with a local KCBS event.

### Abstract: Starting with a local KCBS event is smart - the scale is more manageable and people are generally helpful to newcomers. But I'd strongly recommend attending one as a spectator first or volunteering as a turn-in runner. The logistics of turning in four meats in specific time windows while maintaining quality is the part videos don't fully convey. Some things to know: presentation matters more than you'd expect. The visual appearance in the turn-in box (nine pieces per category, specific box requirements) affects scoring significantly. Competition presentation is a skill unto itself. Timing is everything - each meat category has a specific turn-in window (usually 30 minutes). If you miss the window, you're disqualified for that category. Managing the cook schedules across four meats while one pit might run hot is stressful. Flavor profiles lean sweeter and more pronounced than backyard BBQ - judges taste 20-30 samples per category, so yours needs to pop in one or two bites. Since you're strong on ribs and pork, consider starting with a chicken/rib two-meat competition if your area has them. Lower complexity while you learn the logistics.

### Human: The judging criteria worry me - I cook for flavor but I don't really know what competition judges are looking for. How do I figure that out?

### Abstract: Competition judging is more specific than "tastes good." KCBS criteria: appearance (how it looks in the box - color, uniformity, arrangement), taste (flavor, balance of sweet/savory/heat, smoke), and tenderness (proper texture for each meat - ribs shouldn't fall off the bone despite popular belief, should pull clean with a slight tug). To understand judging: become a certified KCBS judge. Take the CBJ class (about $50, one day). You'll learn the scoring rubric, taste samples, and understand what judges are trained to look for. This is the single most valuable thing you can do before competing. Join a competition BBQ forum or Facebook group (BBQ Brethren, competition-specific groups). People share feedback on what's scoring well, flavor profile trends, and common mistakes. Cook for people who compete - find local competitors and ask if you can trade cooks or get feedback. The competition scene is surprisingly collegial. Practice turn-in box presentation - this is a specific skill. Watch videos on garnish placement, meat arrangement, and what makes an eye-catching box. Judges don't eat the parsley, but it frames your meat.
