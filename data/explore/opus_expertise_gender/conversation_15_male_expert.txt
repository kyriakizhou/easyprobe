### Human: I'm the head of quantitative research at a systematic macro hedge fund. We're exploring incorporating alternative data into our models - specifically satellite imagery and credit card transaction data. What's your framework for evaluating whether alternative data sources actually add alpha?

### Assistant: Alternative data evaluation requires rigorous methodology because the space is filled with spurious correlations, overfitting, and data that looks predictive in backtests but fails live. Framework for evaluation: theoretical prior: before looking at any data, articulate why this data source should predict what you're trying to predict. Satellite imagery of parking lots predicts retail sales because foot traffic correlates with revenue - there's a causal mechanism. If you can't articulate a plausible mechanism, you're likely data mining. Signal-to-noise assessment: alternative data is typically noisy. Assess the fundamental signal quality. For satellite imagery: what's the coverage, frequency, and accuracy? How much is obscured by weather? For credit card data: what's the panel representativeness? What fraction of total spend does it capture? Biases in the panel can create systematic errors. Backtest design rigor: alternative data backtests are prone to severe overfitting. Requirements: true out-of-sample testing (not just hold-out, but data that wasn't available when you designed the signal), point-in-time accuracy (use data as it would have been available, not revised data), realistic latency assumptions (when would you actually receive this data?), transaction cost and capacity analysis. Incremental value over existing signals: the question isn't whether satellite data predicts retail sales, it's whether it predicts retail sales better than or differently from what you already have. If your existing models already incorporate similar information through other channels, the marginal value may be limited. What specific strategies or asset classes are you looking to enhance with these data sources?

### Human: We're primarily focused on equity factor models and macro trading. For equities, we're looking at whether satellite and transaction data can improve our earnings surprise predictions. For macro, we're exploring whether transaction data can give us earlier reads on economic indicators like retail sales and consumer spending.

### Abstract: Those are two of the most validated use cases for alternative data, which is good - you're not fishing in speculative waters. But the space is now crowded, which has implications. Earnings surprise prediction with alternative data: the thesis is sound - satellite foot traffic and credit card spend provide real-time revenue signals before quarterly reports. Key considerations: crowding effects: this signal has been exploited for 5+ years by sophisticated quants. The alpha has likely decayed significantly as more funds trade on similar signals. You need to assess whether there's still exploitable edge, or whether the information is now priced efficiently. Company coverage: satellite and transaction data cover a subset of the investable universe - primarily consumer-facing companies with physical presence or card-based transactions. Your signal will be concentrated in retail, restaurants, airlines, hotels. Does this fit your portfolio construction? Noise around earnings: even accurate revenue signals don't perfectly predict earnings surprises. Margin variability, one-time items, and guidance dynamics all add noise. The signal improves your odds but doesn't eliminate uncertainty. Macro trading with transaction data: real-time economic indicators are valuable for macro trading, particularly around data releases. Considerations: revision dynamics: official statistics get revised. Your transaction data doesn't match the first release methodology exactly, so you're predicting a noisy signal. Understand the relationship between your alternative data and how the official statistics are constructed. Latency advantage: how much earlier do you get signal versus the official release? If it's days, that's tradeable. If it's hours, the value is primarily in positioning around the release itself. Panel representativeness: credit card panels have demographic and geographic biases. Extrapolating to aggregate economy requires careful adjustment. Evaluation process I'd recommend: negotiate trial access to data, run controlled backtests with rigorous out-of-sample methodology, paper trade the signals for 3-6 months before allocating capital, and monitor for signal decay once live.

### Human: On the crowding concern for earnings prediction - how do you assess whether an alternative data signal has been arbitraged away versus still having exploitable alpha? This seems hard to evaluate before committing significant resources to data licensing.

### Abstract: Assessing signal decay without full data access is genuinely challenging, but there are approaches that provide evidence before major commitment. Pre-licensing assessment approaches: academic and practitioner research: the existence of published research on a signal is a warning sign. If there are papers demonstrating alpha from satellite-based retail predictions, that alpha has likely decayed since publication. Look for research publication dates relative to data availability - signals published 3+ years after data became available are more likely to be arbitraged. Vendor client base analysis: data vendors sometimes disclose client counts or types. If a vendor has sold their satellite retail data to 50+ systematic funds, crowding is likely severe. Ask vendors directly (they may not answer), or infer from marketing materials and conference presence. Market efficiency tests: without the specific data, you can test related hypotheses. If satellite data is successfully predicting earnings surprises, you'd expect: surprise magnitude to be smaller for covered stocks (the information is being traded in), pre-earnings drift patterns to differ for high-coverage versus low-coverage stocks. You can run these tests with public data. Decay curve analysis from vendors: sophisticated vendors sometimes provide decay analysis showing how signal strength has evolved over time. Request this - though be skeptical as vendors have incentive to minimize decay concerns. Pilot before full commitment: negotiate limited trials - geographic subset, sector subset, or time-limited access at reduced cost. Use the pilot to validate your backtest methodology and get preliminary signal quality reads. If the vendor refuses any trial, that's informative. Signal decomposition: alternative data often combines truly novel information with information that's available through other channels. Before licensing, understand what portion of the signal could be replicated with cheaper or free data. The unique component is what you're paying for. Structural alpha versus statistical alpha: signals based on genuine information advantages (faster data, better coverage) may persist longer than signals based on statistical patterns that can be replicated once known. Satellite imagery of retailers has structural elements (you literally see the parking lots) that make complete arbitrage harder than purely statistical signals.
