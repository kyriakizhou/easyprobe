### Human: I've been doing yoga for about 3 years and recently started teaching. Some students ask about modifications for injuries, but I don't feel confident advising them. How do I handle this responsibly?

### Assistant: That's a really thoughtful concern, and it shows you take your responsibility as a teacher seriously. The key is knowing the boundaries of your expertise. You're qualified to offer general modifications for common limitations, but specific injury rehabilitation should involve healthcare professionals. What types of injuries are students most often asking about?

### Human: Mostly knee issues, lower back pain, and shoulder problems. I know basic modifications but I'm never sure how much to push or what to avoid entirely.

### Assistant: Those are the big three! For knees: offer props under knees in low lunge, allow bent knees in forward folds, suggest avoiding deep squats or hero pose variations. For low back: emphasize neutral spine, offer bent-knee options, avoid deep backbends and twists without proper engagement. For shoulders: straps for binds, blocks to reduce range of motion, avoiding weight-bearing if acute. The universal guidance: if it causes pain, stop or modify further. You're not treating the injury - you're working around it safely.

### Human: What if I give a modification and they still have pain? I worry about making things worse.

### Assistant: That's the right instinct! Your role is to offer alternatives, not diagnose or guarantee outcomes. If a student reports continued pain even with modification, the appropriate response is: "I'd recommend resting that pose today and consulting with a physical therapist or doctor who can assess your specific situation." You're not failing them - you're being responsible about scope of practice. Some poses may simply not be appropriate for certain injuries right now, and that's okay. Always err on the side of caution.

### Human: Should I be asking students about injuries before class? I've seen some teachers do intake forms.

### Assistant: Intake forms are excellent practice, especially for private sessions or regular students! For drop-in classes, asking at the start "Anyone dealing with injuries or conditions I should know about?" is standard. Keep responses confidential and offer to chat briefly before class. This protects both you and students. Some teachers also add liability waivers, which your studio may require. Documentation shows you're taking reasonable precautions. Consider also building a relationship with local PTs who understand yoga - they can be valuable resources for referrals and learning.

### Human: Any recommendations for deepening my knowledge about working with injuries?

### Assistant: Great instinct to keep learning! Look into yoga therapy training (IAYT-accredited programs), anatomy workshops specific to yoga, or continuing education focused on specific populations. Books like "Yoga for Wellness" by Gary Kraftsow or anything by Judith Lasater on therapeutic applications are valuable. Taking classes from teachers who specialize in adaptive yoga teaches a lot. Some teachers pursue PT or massage therapy certifications to deepen their anatomical knowledge. But remember: even with more training, you're a yoga teacher, not a clinician. The goal is to teach safer, more informed yoga, not to treat injuries.
