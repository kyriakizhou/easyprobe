### Human: I'm the head of treasury at a multinational corporation with significant emerging market exposure. We're seeing increased volatility in several of our key EM currencies and I'm trying to think through our hedging strategy. What framework do you use for EM currency risk management?

### Assistant: EM currency risk management requires a different framework than developed market FX hedging due to several unique characteristics: limited or expensive hedge availability, higher volatility regimes, and correlation with operational risks (EM currency crises often coincide with demand shocks). Framework dimensions: exposure categorization: distinguish between transaction exposure (contracted cash flows in foreign currency), translation exposure (consolidation of foreign subsidiary results), and economic exposure (competitive position impact from currency moves). These require different treatment. Transaction exposure is most hedgeable; translation exposure hedging is often accounting-driven; economic exposure is hardest to hedge directly. Cost-benefit of hedging: EM hedge costs can be substantial - forward points reflecting interest rate differentials, option premiums elevated by higher implied volatility. Sometimes the hedge cost exceeds reasonable expected loss. Calculate break-even: what currency move is the hedge protecting against, and is that move's probability sufficient to justify the cost? Natural hedge potential: before financial hedging, examine operational hedges. Can you match revenue and cost currencies? Can you price in local currency with escalation clauses? Can you adjust supply chain to create offsets? These are often more efficient than financial hedges for long-term structural exposure. Which specific EM currencies represent your largest exposures, and is the concern primarily transaction exposure or are you dealing with significant translation or economic exposure?

### Human: Our largest exposures are in Brazilian real, Turkish lira, and Indonesian rupiah. The exposure is mixed - we have significant revenue in local currency in Brazil and Indonesia, but costs are partly dollar-denominated, so there's meaningful transaction exposure. Turkey is mainly translation exposure from a local subsidiary. The recent lira volatility is particularly concerning.

### Abstract: Those three currencies represent different hedging challenges and strategies. Brazil (BRL): Brazil has a well-developed derivatives market. You can hedge with deliverable forwards, NDFs, and options. The carry cost is high due to Selic rates, but liquidity is good. For your situation (local revenue, partial USD costs), the transaction exposure is asymmetric - BRL depreciation hurts you. Consider: rolling forward program for contracted USD outflows, options if you want asymmetric protection (put spreads can reduce premium cost), and examining whether you can increase local sourcing to create natural offset. Turkey (TRY): Turkey is the highest risk currency of the three - structural current account deficit, unconventional monetary policy, and lira has depreciated roughly 90% against USD over the past decade. For translation exposure, many companies choose not to hedge because: hedge costs are extremely high (lira rates vs. USD), the hedge is just a P&L timing play (doesn't change economics), and lira weakness often correlates with local asset value decline (you'd be hedging against depreciation while also losing on the underlying). Consider: do you really need to hedge translation, or can you manage via subsidiary capital structure (minimize net assets exposed)? If translation volatility affects covenants or analyst perception, that changes the calculus. Indonesia (IDR): Indonesia is moderate volatility, reasonable liquidity via NDF market. Similar structure to Brazil for you - local revenue, partial USD costs. IDR is less volatile than BRL or TRY but can move sharply during risk-off environments. NDF hedging is the primary instrument; options exist but with wider bid-ask. Overall strategy question: what's your risk tolerance and how do you measure it? Hedge ratios, time horizons, and instrument choices flow from how you define acceptable exposure.

### Human: On Turkey specifically - you're right that we're questioning whether to hedge translation at all given the costs. But our CFO is concerned about quarterly earnings volatility from TRY translation. Is there a way to manage the perception issue without paying prohibitive hedge costs?

### Abstract: The tension between actual economics and earnings perception is common with EM translation exposure. Several approaches to manage perception without expensive hedging: financial structuring to reduce translation exposure: minimize net asset exposure in Turkey. This means: keeping Turkish subsidiary leverage high (local debt offsets local assets), dividend policy that repatriates cash regularly rather than accumulating local currency assets, minimizing intercompany receivables denominated in TRY. This reduces the balance sheet item that creates translation volatility. You're not hedging - you're reducing the underlying exposure structurally. Investor communication strategy: rather than hiding volatility, educate investors to focus on constant-currency operating results. Report both as-reported and constant-currency metrics prominently. Most sophisticated investors already adjust for EM FX in their models. The quarterly volatility matters less if investors understand and expect it. Designate net investment hedge: if you have USD debt at the parent level, you can potentially designate a portion as a net investment hedge of your Turkish subsidiary. The FX impact on the debt then flows through OCI rather than P&L, offsetting some of the translation effect. This is an accounting treatment, not economic hedging, but it can reduce earnings volatility. Partial hedging at opportunistic levels: rather than a systematic hedge program, consider tactical hedges when TRY strengthens and hedge costs are temporarily lower. This requires a view on currency direction, which is risky, but may be sensible if hedge costs are prohibitive at current levels. CFO communication: help your CFO frame this for the board and analysts. The choice is between predictable P&L drag (hedge cost) and episodic translation losses. At current TRY hedge costs, the predictable drag may exceed expected translation losses. This is a choice about which earnings pattern the market prefers, not necessarily an economic optimization.
