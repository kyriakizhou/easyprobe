### Human: I've been doing calligraphy as a hobby for about 4 years and I've gotten pretty good. Friends keep suggesting I sell my work or teach, but I don't know if there's really a market. Is hand lettering still relevant in the digital age?

### Assistant: Absolutely! The digital age has actually increased demand for handmade, artisanal touches - calligraphy for weddings, events, and branding stands out precisely because everything else is digital. The market segments include: wedding and event calligraphy (invitations, envelopes, place cards, signage), commercial work (logos, branding, product lettering), teaching (workshops, online courses), and original art pieces. What styles do you work in, and what do you most enjoy creating?

### Human: Mostly pointed pen scripts - Copperplate and modern calligraphy. I've done wedding invitations for friends and really enjoyed it. I'm faster than I was but hand-addressing still takes forever.

### Assistant: Pointed pen and wedding work is a natural pairing and sustainable market! The speed issue is real - envelope addressing is time-consuming and needs to be priced accordingly. Many wedding calligraphers charge $3-8 per envelope depending on market and complexity, which seems high until you factor in time. Build efficiency by: developing a rhythm rather than treating each envelope as art, using guide sheets for consistency, and accepting that speed increases with repetition. For the business side, wedding calligraphy benefits from vendor relationships - planners, stationers, and photographers who can refer.

### Human: How do I actually find clients? I've only done work for people I know.

### Assistant: Building from your network is the right starting point - those friends can refer, and you can use that work for portfolio. Beyond that: create a focused Instagram presence showcasing wedding work (wedding industry lives on Instagram and Pinterest), get listed on wedding directories (The Knot, WeddingWire), reach out to local wedding planners with samples, build relationships with stationery designers who might outsource addressing, and attend bridal shows or styled shoots. Word of mouth is powerful in weddings - one happy bride tells her engaged friends. A professional website with clear pricing, turnaround times, and booking process helps convert inquiries.

### Human: What about teaching? I've thought about workshops but don't know if I'm qualified to teach.

### Assistant: If you can do it well, you can teach it! Teaching doesn't require formal credentials - it requires knowledge, clear communication, and patience. Your four years of practice and the quality of your work are your qualifications. Start with: beginner workshops at local venues (craft stores, community centers, wine bars), private small-group sessions, or one-on-one instruction. Keep workshops simple at first (maybe 2-3 hours on a specific script), have samples and supplies ready, and over-prepare. Many calligraphers find teaching rewarding and profitable - workshop income can be more predictable than commissioned work. It also builds your reputation and drives commissioned work from students.

### Human: How do I price commissioned work? I have no idea what's fair.

### Assistant: Research your local market - look at what other calligraphers charge. General ranges: envelope addressing $3-8 each, invitation suite calligraphy $300-800+, place cards $2-5 each, signage $75-200+ depending on size. Price based on your time plus materials plus profit margin, not just "what feels okay." Track how long things actually take you and set an hourly floor you won't go below. It's better to price properly and get fewer clients than undervalue your work and burn out. New calligraphers often underprice; remember that you're offering skilled handwork that takes years to develop. Increase prices as demand and skill grow.
