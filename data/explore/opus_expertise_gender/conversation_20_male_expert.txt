### Human: I'm the CEO of a mid-size defense contractor. We're looking at opportunities in the commercial space sector as an adjacency to our core government contracting business. What's your perspective on how defense primes should think about commercial space?

### Assistant: Defense contractors entering commercial space face a fundamental culture and business model transition that many underestimate. The capabilities may overlap, but the operating environments are quite different. Key tensions to navigate: cost structure: defense contracting typically operates on cost-plus or fixed-price contracts where the customer absorbs significant development risk and accepts higher unit costs for mission assurance. Commercial space (especially launch and satellite constellations) demands dramatically lower costs - sometimes 10x reductions. Your existing cost structure, overhead rates, and supplier relationships may not translate. Speed and iteration: defense programs operate on multi-year development cycles with extensive requirements definition upfront. Commercial space, particularly the New Space ecosystem, iterates rapidly with MVP approaches and accepts higher failure rates. Organizational culture optimized for defense risk management often struggles to move at commercial speed. Customer concentration versus diversification: your government business has concentrated customers with deep relationships. Commercial space may require building sales and marketing capabilities you don't currently have, serving customers with different procurement styles and expectations. Risk tolerance: commercial space ventures, especially early stage, have different risk profiles than government contracts. How does your board and shareholder base view investing in higher-risk commercial ventures? What's your current government space exposure and what specific commercial opportunities are you evaluating?

### Human: We have significant government space business - satellite subsystems and ground equipment primarily. We're seeing commercial constellation operators and launch providers as potential customers for similar products. The question is whether we can serve them at price points that work, and whether we should pursue this through our existing organization or set up something separate.

### Abstract: Your positioning - existing space subsystems and ground equipment with potential commercial customers - is one of the more tractable entry points into commercial space. You're not trying to become a launch company or constellation operator; you're extending your product sales to new customer segments. Organizational structure question: the separate organization versus integrated approach is critical and depends on how different the commercial offering needs to be. Arguments for integrated approach: products are technically similar, you can leverage existing engineering and production capabilities, sales and BD can be extended to new customers, avoiding duplication of overhead. Arguments for separate entity: commercial requires different cost structure that's hard to achieve within existing overhead (DCAA-compliant systems add cost that commercial customers won't pay), faster decision-making and iteration, different talent profile (commercially-oriented versus government-oriented), cleaner accounting and performance measurement, easier to partner or take outside investment if needed. Hybrid recommendation: consider a separate commercial unit with shared technical resources but independent commercial infrastructure. The engineering and manufacturing capabilities can be leveraged (avoiding duplication), but sales, pricing, contract management, and product management should be commercial-native. This unit should have clear P&L accountability and different success metrics than your government business. Pricing feasibility assessment: before committing, do bottoms-up analysis of what it would take to serve commercial customers profitably. Strip out government-specific overhead, assess whether your supply chain can deliver at commercial price points, evaluate whether your product specifications are over-engineered for commercial needs. The answer determines whether this is viable within your current structure or requires fundamental changes.

### Human: The cost structure question is the crux. Our current overhead rates reflect our government business infrastructure. How do we practically create a commercial cost structure without undermining the government business or creating compliance issues with our government contracts?

### Abstract: This is the central challenge for defense contractors pursuing commercial business, and it requires careful structuring to avoid both operational dysfunction and regulatory risk. The compliance dimension: DCAA and contracting officers scrutinize contractors who appear to be shifting costs inappropriately between government and commercial work. Key concerns: ensuring government contracts bear their fair share of overhead (not subsidizing commercial ventures with government money), ensuring commercial work isn't allocated costs that should flow to commercial customers, maintaining consistent cost accounting across the business. Practical structuring approaches: separate legal entity: the cleanest approach is a distinct subsidiary for commercial work. It has its own cost accounting system, its own overhead pools, and arms-length transactions with the parent. This creates clear separation but requires genuine operational independence - not just paper separation. Regulators will look through structures that lack substance. Dual overhead pools: within a single entity, you can maintain separate overhead pools for government and commercial work if they genuinely serve different customer bases with different cost drivers. This requires robust cost accounting to allocate fairly and consistently. It's less clean than separate entities but can work. Direct cost model for commercial: for specific commercial products, identify which costs are truly direct (can be specifically identified with the commercial work) versus indirect. Commercial pricing should be based on direct costs plus a commercial-appropriate overhead allocation, not your blended government rate structure. Supply chain optimization: a significant portion of government product cost comes from DFARS-compliant supply chain requirements, quality assurance overhead, and mil-spec components. Commercial products may not need all of these. Developing a commercial supply chain can substantially reduce product costs without changing your internal structure. Practical recommendation: before restructuring, do the detailed analysis. Build should-cost models for your commercial product opportunity assuming commercial-appropriate overhead rates and supply chain. If the math works with reasonable margins, the structure is worth figuring out. If you need 50% cost reduction and your inherent manufacturing costs are only 20% lower than current product cost, no structure will solve the problem.
