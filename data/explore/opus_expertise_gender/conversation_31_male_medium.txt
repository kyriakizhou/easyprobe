### Human: I've been a software developer for about 5 years and I'm starting to hit a plateau in salary increases at my current company. I'm wondering if I need to job hop to get to the next level or if there's something else I should be doing.

### Assistant: The salary plateau after 5 years at one company is common and often real - internal raises rarely keep pace with market movement for skilled developers. However, whether job hopping is the right move depends on several factors: what's your current compensation relative to market? Are you being underpaid, at market, or above market? What's your role trajectory - are you on track for senior, staff, or management if you want those? And is the plateau about money specifically, or also about growth, challenge, or learning? Sometimes people conflate salary stagnation with broader career stagnation.

### Human: I think I'm a bit below market based on levels.fyi and talking to friends at other companies. Role-wise I'm a senior dev but there's not a clear path to staff at my company. It's mostly about money but I'm also getting bored with the tech stack.

### Abstract: Below market, no clear path to staff, and bored with the tech stack - that's a strong case for looking externally. The combination suggests you've extracted most of the value this position offers. Job hopping math: developers who change jobs every 2-3 years typically out-earn those who stay, often significantly (20-40% increases versus 3-5% annual raises). The main costs are lost institutional knowledge, restarting relationships, and the effort of interviewing. At 5 years, you've likely already paid the "stability" tax that benefits employers more than employees. Approach: start interviewing while employed. This gives you leverage and removes desperation from the equation. You can be selective. Target companies with clear staff engineer tracks if that's your goal, and tech stacks that interest you. Even if you decide to stay, going through the interview process updates your market value knowledge and often lights a fire to negotiate internally. One consideration: before fully committing to leaving, have you explicitly discussed path to staff with your manager? Sometimes companies have options they haven't communicated. A direct conversation - "What would it take for me to reach staff level here, and on what timeline?" - gives you information. If the answer is vague or unrealistic, you have your answer.

### Human: I haven't had that explicit conversation yet. I've hinted at wanting more but never directly asked. Is that the kind of thing you should just come out and ask?

### Abstract: Yes, be direct. Hinting doesn't work because managers have plausible deniability about understanding what you want, and they're not incentivized to proactively offer you more money or bigger titles. The direct conversation: schedule a 1:1 specifically for career development (not your regular status update). Frame it: "I want to discuss my career trajectory here. I'm interested in growing to staff engineer level. What would that path look like at this company, and what do I need to demonstrate to get there?" Listen carefully to the response. Good signs: specific criteria, realistic timeline, concrete next steps. Bad signs: vague platitudes ("keep doing great work"), mention of budget constraints or organizational challenges without path forward, timeline that's indefinite. If the path seems real, you can also ask about compensation: "My research suggests I'm somewhat below market for my role and experience. What options do we have to address that?" Some companies can and will adjust. Others can't or won't. Both are valuable information. This conversation has almost no downside. Either you get a path forward that satisfies you (win), or you confirm what you suspected and have clear justification to job search (also a win). The main risk - being perceived as a flight risk - is somewhat mitigated by framing it as wanting to grow there, not threatening to leave.
