### Human: I'm designing a competency framework for evaluating surgical residents that incorporates procedural skill assessment. Traditional milestone evaluations feel inadequate for technical skill, but objective measurement of surgical competence is challenging. What approaches are you seeing?

### Assistant: Surgical competency assessment is evolving significantly. Traditional milestone approaches capture knowledge and general competencies but struggle with procedural skill which is multidimensional - technique, efficiency, decision-making under uncertainty, complication management. Emerging approaches: objective structured assessment of technical skills (OSATS) with validated global rating scales, simulation-based assessment with standardized scenarios, video review with structured evaluation protocols, and increasingly, AI-assisted analysis of surgical video. The challenge is: any single method has limitations. What's your specialty context and what decisions will the framework inform - formative feedback, promotion decisions, or credentialing?

### Human: Minimally invasive surgery, both formative feedback during training and summative decisions about graduation readiness. We've piloted OSATS but inter-rater reliability is inconsistent and faculty time is limited.

### Assistant: MIS is particularly amenable to objective measurement because of video capture. For inter-rater reliability: structured training for evaluators with calibration sessions and standardized cases improves consistency significantly. Consider anchor videos representing each performance level. But your faculty time constraint is real. Approaches that reduce faculty burden: peer assessment (residents evaluating each other with appropriate training), self-assessment compared to expert benchmark videos, and automated metrics from surgical video (time, motion, efficiency measures). The automated metrics are increasingly validated - studies show motion tracking metrics correlate with expert global ratings. What's your video capture infrastructure?

### Human: We capture most MIS cases but don't have systematic analysis - videos mostly used for M&M review. Moving to routine analysis feels like a big infrastructure investment.

### Assistant: Infrastructure investment decision depends on volume and value. If you're training 15+ residents and performing 1000+ MIS cases annually, the marginal cost of systematic analysis decreases and value of longitudinal data increases. Implementation approach: start with sampling strategy rather than attempting comprehensive analysis - maybe index cases representing key procedures and decision points. Commercial platforms exist (Surgical Safety Technologies, Theator, others) that provide automated video analysis and increasingly sophisticated AI-assisted metrics. Cost-benefit: compare to current faculty assessment time, consider quality improvement and liability management value beyond training, and evaluate research opportunities. You might start with a pilot on a single procedure type to demonstrate value before scaling.

### Human: What about the assessment validity question? How do we know these metrics actually predict clinical competence as independent practitioners?

### Assistant: The validity question is fundamental and incompletely answered. Current evidence: process metrics (efficiency, motion) correlate with expert ratings and distinguish experience levels, but the link to patient outcomes is less well-established. The gold standard would be prospective validation against complication rates in independent practice, but this requires long follow-up and large samples. Reasonable current approach: use multi-method assessment where no single metric determines competence - combine objective procedural metrics with case volume, complication rates during supervised practice, faculty global assessment, and milestone achievement. Triangulation across methods provides confidence even when individual measures have limitations. Also: be transparent with residents about what metrics mean and don't mean, and use for development, not just evaluation.

### Human: How do we handle the resident who performs well in simulation and assessment but whose clinical judgment in real cases is concerning?

### Assistant: This highlights the limits of technical skill assessment - surgical competence includes judgment, communication, and adaptive expertise that don't fully manifest in controlled assessment. For the judgment-concerning resident: more direct observation in real clinical environments (scrubbed-in faculty, OR nursing input), structured case review focused on decision-making process not just outcomes, graduated autonomy with explicit supervision level tied to observed judgment, and potentially simulation of complication scenarios and unexpected findings. Document concerns specifically - "concerning judgment" is too vague for remediation or adverse action. What specific judgment patterns worry you? Failing to recognize complications? Proceeding when should abort? This specificity guides intervention. Sometimes the concern is appropriate caution in a trainee versus problematic hesitancy - ensure your evaluation distinguishes these.
