### Human: I'm working on a complex multiparty contract for a joint venture in renewable energy. We're struggling with the force majeure provisions given climate-related events. Traditional force majeure language feels inadequate. How are you seeing this evolve?

### Assistant: Climate risk is fundamentally reshaping force majeure thinking in energy contracts. Traditional language was designed for rare, truly unforeseeable events - but climate-related disruptions are increasingly foreseeable and systemic. The tension: if events are foreseeable, traditional force majeure doctrine may not apply, but parties still need mechanisms for allocating weather-related risks. Emerging approaches include: graduated response frameworks (delay rights before full excuse), parametric triggers tied to objective weather metrics, and explicit carve-outs for specific climate scenarios with predetermined allocation. What's the structure of your JV - who bears what risks under the base agreement?

### Human: It's a 60/40 JV between a developer and a utility. Developer responsible for construction and initial operations, utility takes over long-term operation. Both parties exposed to generation output risks.

### Assistant: The risk allocation between development phase and operational phase is critical here. During construction, climate events (extreme weather, flooding) can cause delays and cost overruns - who bears that risk and how is it capped? During operations, generation variability affects revenue. For construction: consider weather-day allowances explicitly defined (X days of weather delay included in schedule), insurance requirements for specific perils, and limits on developer's exposure before risk shifts or triggers renegotiation. For operations: typically handled through capacity factor guarantees, availability payments versus energy payments, and insurance products (parametric weather derivatives). The force majeure clause interacts with but doesn't replace these specific allocation mechanisms.

### Human: What about truly extreme events - a Katrina-level hurricane or unprecedented flooding? Neither party wants unlimited exposure but we can't agree on risk caps.

### Assistant: For tail risks, several structuring options: tiered responsibility (each party bears first $X of losses, then shared, then insurance, then renegotiation trigger), industry standard insurance requirements with agreed coverage limits as implicit cap, material adverse change clauses that trigger restructuring discussions rather than unilateral relief, or agreed termination rights with predetermined wind-down provisions. The philosophical challenge: unlimited exposure is unacceptable to both parties, but predetermined caps create moral hazard and strategic behavior. Some JVs use "meet and confer" provisions that require good-faith renegotiation when losses exceed thresholds, rather than automatic outcomes. It postpones resolution but preserves relationship.

### Human: How do we draft triggers that don't become litigation targets? Our counterparty's counsel is very sophisticated.

### Assistant: Specificity reduces litigation risk but also reduces flexibility. Best practices: use objective, verifiable triggers wherever possible (named storms meeting specific criteria, measured precipitation levels, regulatory declarations), define notice procedures and documentation requirements precisely, specify the relief available and its duration, and address partial performance scenarios (what if force majeure affects 30% of the project?). For subjective elements that can't be avoided, consider expert determination or arbitration provisions with expedited timelines - faster resolution than litigation with specialized decision-makers. Also: the commercial relationship matters. Sophisticated counterparties understand that excessive litigation risk increases transaction costs for everyone. A thoughtfully drafted provision with clear dispute resolution mechanism often performs better than attempting to anticipate every scenario.

### Human: Any specific drafting pitfalls you'd flag for this kind of deal?

### Assistant: Several common pitfalls: overly broad force majeure definitions that could be abused to escape underperforming contracts, ambiguity about notice requirements and timing (be specific about what notice is required and when relief begins), failure to address mitigation obligations and what efforts are required before claiming force majeure, silent on what happens after force majeure ends (automatic resumption, renegotiation rights?), and not coordinating with other contractual provisions (insurance requirements, MAC clauses, termination rights). Also consider: if force majeure is claimed, what visibility does the other party have into the claimant's actual situation? Information rights during force majeure events prevent abuse and enable collaborative problem-solving.
