### Human: I've been trading options for about a year and a half, mostly covered calls and cash-secured puts. I want to understand more advanced strategies but the risk seems to jump dramatically. What should I know before going further?

### Assistant: You've started with the safer end of options strategies - covered calls and CSPs have defined risk and you own (or are willing to own) the underlying. The "risk jump" you sense when looking at more advanced strategies is real but often misunderstood. Some advanced strategies (credit spreads, iron condors) actually have more defined risk than selling naked options. Others (selling naked, ratio spreads) can have theoretically unlimited risk. The distinction between them matters enormously. What draws you to more advanced strategies - are you looking for more income, more capital efficiency, or the ability to express different market views?

### Human: Mainly capital efficiency. Selling CSPs ties up a lot of capital for the premium received. I've heard vertical spreads help with that but I don't fully understand the mechanics.

### Abstract: Vertical spreads do improve capital efficiency, and they're a logical next step from CSPs and covered calls. The mechanics: instead of selling a put secured by cash for 100 shares, you sell a put and buy a cheaper put further out of the money. The bought put limits your downside but reduces your premium. Example: stock at $100, you sell the 95 put for $3 and buy the 90 put for $1.20. Net credit is $1.80, but your max loss is the spread width ($5) minus credit received ($1.80) = $3.20. Capital required: $320 per spread versus $9,500 for a CSP at the same strike. The tradeoff: your premium is lower (1.80 versus $3), your profit is capped if the stock rises above your short strike, and you're losing money immediately if the stock approaches your short strike rather than just being in an unrealized loss on CSP. The risk is defined but you can still lose your max loss. This works in your favor when: you want to generate income with less capital, you want to express a directional view with defined risk, or you want to participate in premium selling on higher-priced underlyings that would require too much CSP capital. Are you trading in a margin account currently?

### Human: Yes, margin account. I understand the spread mechanics now, but how do I manage them? What happens near expiration?

### Abstract: Spread management is where discipline matters. Near expiration scenarios: both legs expire worthless (stock above short strike for put spread) - ideal outcome, you keep full credit. Stock between strikes - your short leg is ITM, long leg is OTM. You'll get assigned on the short leg and can exercise your long leg. This often results in max loss, but you can close before expiration to avoid assignment complexity. Stock below both strikes - both legs exercise, offsetting each other. You realize max loss. Management approaches: profit targets - many spread traders close at 50-70% of max profit rather than waiting for expiration. This reduces time exposure and frees capital. If you received $1.80 and it's now worth $0.60, you've captured $1.20 - close and move on. Stop losses - decide beforehand when you'll exit for a loss. Many traders exit when the spread is worth 2x what they received (so $3.60 on a $1.80 credit). This prevents a small loss becoming max loss. Time decay behavior - spreads benefit from theta decay, but the curve isn't linear. Most decay happens in the final two weeks. Be aware of gamma risk (rapid delta changes) near expiration on strikes close to the stock price. Key rule: never let a short leg be in the money near expiration without a plan. Getting assigned on spreads creates complexity. Close positions by expiration Friday morning if there's any doubt.
