### Human: I'm designing organizational structure for a rapidly scaling climate tech company. We've grown from 50 to 200 in 18 months and experiencing significant coordination problems. Classic symptoms - duplicated work, unclear ownership, decisions falling through cracks. How do you approach this?

### Assistant: The 50-to-200 transition is one of the hardest scaling inflection points. At 50, informal coordination works; at 200, you need explicit structure. But the solution isn't just adding hierarchy - it's designing the right coordination mechanisms for your work's nature. Climate tech varies significantly - are you hardware-intensive, software-focused, project-based, or hybrid? The right structure depends heavily on where coordination needs are most critical.

### Human: Hybrid - we develop hardware sensing equipment and software platform for carbon measurement. Hardware team is about 60, software is 80, plus commercial, operations, and G&A. The hardware and software need to work closely but have very different development rhythms.

### Assistant: The hardware/software interface is your critical coordination challenge. Options: functional structure (separate hardware and software orgs with strong cross-functional coordination mechanisms), product-based structure (integrated teams around product lines, with hardware and software mixed), or hybrid matrix (functional homes with project-based integration). Given different development rhythms, pure product teams can be challenging - hardware cycles are longer and require different capital/resource planning. Many successful hardware/software companies use: functional structure with strong product management as coordination layer, regular integration points rather than continuous coordination, and dedicated integration teams at the hardware/software boundary.

### Human: We have product management but they're overwhelmed and treated as coordinators rather than empowered decision-makers. Engineering largely ignores them on roadmap.

### Assistant: That's a common and serious dysfunction. If product management exists but lacks authority, you have responsibility-authority mismatch creating organizational friction. Root causes to diagnose: is the issue PM capability (are they skilled enough to earn engineering respect?), organizational design (do they have explicit decision rights?), or founder behavior (are founders overriding PMs and thereby undermining them?). Fixes depend on root cause. If decision rights are unclear, you need explicit RACI or similar framework - not as bureaucracy but as clarity about who decides what. If engineering ignores product because founders routinely override product, that's a founder behavior issue. If PMs lack capability, that's a hiring/development issue. Which of these resonates?

### Human: Probably all three, honestly. Our founder-CEO remains heavily involved in technical decisions and PMs have become project managers rather than product managers.

### Assistant: This is extremely common in founder-led scaling companies. The founder involvement that made you successful at 50 creates bottlenecks at 200. Several interventions: CEO explicitly transfers decision rights to PMs in defined areas (not all decisions, but clear categories), CEO role evolves to setting strategy and reviewing outcomes rather than making operational decisions, PMs are upgraded or replaced with people who can hold decision authority credibly, and organizational mechanisms force decisions to happen without CEO involvement (decision deadlines, escalation only for defined criteria). This is fundamentally about CEO letting go of certain decisions - structurally easy, emotionally hard. Have you had direct conversation with your CEO about this dynamic?

### Human: We've discussed but it keeps reverting. Part of it is that CEO doesn't trust the current team to make good decisions without oversight.

### Assistant: If the trust issue is legitimate - the team genuinely makes poor decisions without oversight - then structure won't solve it; you have a talent or capability problem that structure is compensating for. If the trust issue is CEO difficulty delegating - the team is capable but CEO can't let go - then you need coaching/board intervention. Often it's both: team has capability gaps AND CEO has delegation challenges, each reinforcing the other. Breaking the cycle often requires: explicit capability building (better hiring, management training, decision frameworks), graduated delegation (CEO releases smaller decisions, team proves capability, earns more authority), and external accountability (board, executive coach) for the CEO transition. This is one of the hardest founder scaling challenges. What's your board's engagement on organizational development?
