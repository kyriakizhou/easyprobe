### Human: I'm a staff software engineer working on distributed systems at a large tech company. We're designing a new event streaming platform to replace our aging Kafka deployment. Leadership wants us to evaluate both Kafka 3.x and Pulsar. What's your take on the technical tradeoffs at scale?

### Assistant: The Kafka versus Pulsar comparison at scale involves several architectural differences that matter depending on your specific requirements. Key architectural differences: storage architecture: Kafka ties partitions to brokers - data lives on the broker that owns the partition. Pulsar separates compute (brokers) from storage (BookKeeper), allowing independent scaling. At very large scale, Pulsar's architecture can be advantageous for elasticity and rebalancing, but it adds operational complexity with the BookKeeper layer. Tiered storage: both support tiered storage now, but Pulsar's implementation is more mature. If you have significant retention requirements and cost sensitivity about hot storage, this matters. Multi-tenancy: Pulsar was designed with multi-tenancy as a first-class concept. If you need strong isolation between teams or use cases, Pulsar's tenant/namespace model is more robust than Kafka's topic-based ACLs. Operational complexity: Kafka's operational model is well-understood with deep ecosystem tooling. Pulsar requires managing ZooKeeper (though moving away from this), BookKeeper, and brokers - more components, more failure modes, steeper learning curve. Ecosystem: Kafka's ecosystem is significantly more mature - Connect, Streams, Schema Registry, monitoring tools, client libraries. Pulsar is catching up but there are gaps. What specific pain points are you experiencing with your current Kafka deployment that's driving the replacement?

### Human: Main pain points are: partition rebalancing is disruptive and slow, we're struggling with multi-tenancy and resource isolation between teams, and our retention costs are becoming significant. We're running about 500 brokers across multiple clusters processing around 50 million messages per second.

### Abstract: At your scale (500 brokers, 50M msg/sec), those pain points are significant and worth addressing. Let me assess how each platform addresses them: partition rebalancing: this is a genuine Kafka weakness at scale. Rebalancing is I/O intensive and can impact latency during the process. Kafka 3.x improvements help but don't fundamentally change the architecture. Pulsar's separated storage model means broker scaling doesn't require data movement - you can add/remove brokers without partition rebalancing. This is a meaningful advantage at your scale. However, BookKeeper itself requires careful capacity planning and has its own operational considerations. Multi-tenancy and resource isolation: Pulsar has clear advantages here. The namespace model with quotas (rate limiting, storage limits) and isolation policies provides better resource management than Kafka's broker-level configurations. For a large organization with multiple teams, Pulsar's model reduces the "noisy neighbor" problems. Kafka's multi-tenancy typically requires cluster proliferation, which creates operational overhead and underutilization. Retention costs: Pulsar's tiered storage is more mature and the architecture lends itself to efficient cold storage integration. Kafka's tiered storage (KIP-405) is improving but still has rough edges at scale. If you're looking at years of retention, the cost delta can be significant. Given these specific pain points, Pulsar addresses them more directly than Kafka 3.x. But counter-considerations: migration complexity from Kafka to Pulsar at 50M msg/sec is substantial, operational expertise doesn't transfer directly, and ecosystem tooling gaps may require additional investment. What's your organization's operational maturity and appetite for managing BookKeeper?

### Human: We have a strong infrastructure team and manage several distributed systems already, so operational complexity is manageable. My bigger concern is the migration path - we can't afford significant downtime and we have hundreds of consumer applications. How would you approach this migration?

### Abstract: Zero-downtime migration at your scale requires a carefully staged approach. The pattern I'd recommend: phase 1 - dual-write infrastructure: deploy Pulsar infrastructure in parallel with existing Kafka. Set up Kafka-to-Pulsar mirroring using Pulsar's Kafka protocol compatibility or a custom connector. This creates a shadow deployment where Pulsar receives all messages but isn't serving production reads. Use this phase to validate Pulsar performance characteristics match or exceed Kafka at your load, build operational muscle with the new system, identify and resolve issues before they impact consumers. Duration: 4-8 weeks of parallel operation with increasing load validation. Phase 2 - consumer migration waves: migrate consumers in waves, starting with least critical applications. Approach for each consumer: deploy Pulsar consumer alongside Kafka consumer (dual-read), validate message parity and consumer behavior, shift traffic gradually from Kafka consumer to Pulsar, decommission Kafka consumer. Key tooling need: build a consumer migration framework that handles dual-read configuration, parity validation, and traffic shifting. Don't make each team figure this out independently. Categorize consumers by criticality and migrate in order of increasing criticality. Phase 3 - producer migration: once consumers are migrated, shift producers from Kafka to Pulsar. This is actually simpler than consumer migration because you're changing the write path once, not maintaining dual consumption. You can use Pulsar's Kafka protocol compatibility as an intermediate step - producers continue using Kafka clients but write to Pulsar. Phase 4 - Kafka decommission: once all producers and consumers are migrated, run Kafka in receive-only mode for rollback safety, then decommission. Timeline reality: at 500 brokers and hundreds of consumers, plan for 12-18 months for full migration. Rushing introduces risk. The investment in migration tooling and automation pays dividends.
