### Human: I'm a constitutional law professor consulted on legislative language for AI governance. The current draft attempts to define "high-risk AI systems" but the definition is either too narrow (missing concerning applications) or too broad (capturing benign uses). How are you seeing this definitional challenge addressed elsewhere?

### Assistant: The high-risk AI definitional challenge is central to effective AI governance - too narrow allows regulatory arbitrage and misses harms; too broad creates compliance burden that slows beneficial innovation. The EU AI Act took a tiered approach with enumerated high-risk categories (critical infrastructure, education, employment, etc.) plus a catch-all for systems with significant impact on fundamental rights. This combines specificity with flexibility but creates uncertainty at boundaries. Alternative approaches: focus on use cases rather than technology characteristics, define risk based on potential consequences rather than technical implementation, or establish regulatory sandbox mechanisms where classification can be determined case-by-case. What's your legislative context - federal, state, sector-specific?

### Human: State-level general-purpose legislation. We're trying to be technology-neutral but the enumerated approach feels like we'll always be behind. The technology-neutral language we've drafted keeps getting challenged as too vague for enforcement.

### Assistant: The technology-neutral versus specificity tension is real. One approach: define risk based on decision impact rather than technology. "Automated decision systems that make or substantially influence consequential decisions regarding natural persons" - then define "consequential" (employment, credit, housing, criminal justice, healthcare access, education). This captures AI systems that matter without requiring technical definitions that obsolesce. The challenge shifts to defining "consequential" and "substantially influence" - but these are more stable concepts than technical definitions. You also need safe harbors: low-risk uses, uses with meaningful human review, or small-scale applications that shouldn't bear compliance burden.

### Human: The "meaningful human review" concept is contentious. Industry argues any human in the loop satisfies it; advocates argue that's rubber-stamping. How do we operationalize this?

### Assistant: "Meaningful human review" is one of the hardest governance concepts to operationalize. Weak versions: human can theoretically override but practically never does, or human reviews so many decisions they can't meaningfully evaluate any. Strong versions specify: human must have genuine authority to override, must have information necessary to evaluate (not just trust the system output), must have realistic capacity to review (number of decisions, time available), and there must be accountability for human decision-maker. Operationalizing: require documentation of review procedures, statistical analysis of override rates, and human reviewer training requirements. Some frameworks distinguish between "human-in-the-loop" (human must approve each decision), "human-on-the-loop" (human monitors and can intervene), and "human-in-command" (human retains full control). Each appropriate for different risk levels.

### Human: What about enforcement mechanisms? Private right of action versus regulatory agency versus both?

### Assistant: Enforcement mechanism choice has significant implications. Private right of action: provides individual remedies, enables decentralized enforcement, but can lead to strategic litigation and inconsistent application. Regulatory agency: expertise development, consistent standards, but resource-constrained and potentially captured. Most effective frameworks combine both: agency sets standards and brings enforcement for systemic violations, private rights for individual harms with possible administrative exhaustion requirements. Also consider: whistleblower protections for those who report violations, audit requirements with third-party or government access, and graduated penalties that distinguish negligent from intentional violations. Class action availability matters significantly for AI systems that affect many individuals with small individual harms.

### Human: The preemption question keeps coming up - we're concerned about creating a patchwork if other states act differently. Thoughts on federal-state coordination?

### Assistant: Preemption is a live question in AI governance. Arguments for state preemption by federal law: uniformity for companies operating nationally, avoidance of race to the bottom or regulatory arbitrage, and coherent national AI strategy. Arguments against: states as laboratories for experimentation, responsiveness to local concerns and political will, and federal action may be slow or captured. Current reality: no comprehensive federal AI law exists, so states acting fills a vacuum. Options: draft with explicit conditional preemption (state law yields if federal law later addresses same issues), coordinate with other states on model legislation (similar to Uniform Commercial Code approach), or include sunset provisions that force revisitation. The patchwork concern is legitimate but often overstated - companies already navigate state-level variation in privacy, employment, and consumer protection law.
