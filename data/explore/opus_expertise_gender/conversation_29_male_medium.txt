### Human: I've been teaching myself piano for about 3 years using apps and YouTube. I can play some intermediate pieces but I feel like my technique isn't really improving anymore. Should I get a teacher at this point?

### Assistant: The plateau you're describing is common for self-taught musicians around the 2-3 year mark. Apps and YouTube can teach you what to play but often don't address how you're playing - posture, hand position, finger independence, arm weight usage, tension habits. These technique issues become limiting factors that prevent further progress and can even cause injury over time. A good teacher observes things you can't see or feel yourself. The answer is probably yes to getting a teacher, but the specifics matter. What kind of music are you playing, what does your practice look like, and do you have specific goals for the instrument?

### Human: I play mostly classical pieces - some Bach, Chopin nocturnes, that level. I practice about 30-45 minutes most days. Goal is to eventually play more advanced repertoire, maybe some Liszt or Rachmaninoff someday. I notice tension in my forearms sometimes after playing.

### Abstract: The tension you're experiencing is a significant red flag that validates getting a teacher. Playing Chopin with forearm tension suggests technique issues that will absolutely prevent you from progressing to Liszt or Rachmaninoff, which are physically demanding. More importantly, chronic tension causes repetitive strain injuries - pianists develop tendinitis, carpal tunnel, and other problems that can end playing entirely. This alone is reason enough to get proper instruction. For your repertoire goals: Bach and Chopin nocturnes are reasonable intermediate material. Liszt and Rachmaninoff require significantly more technical facility - arm weight, octave technique, finger strength, and endurance that can't be developed with tension habits. A teacher can diagnose what's causing your tension (likely gripping, insufficient arm weight, poor posture, or inefficient fingering) and give you exercises and awareness to fix it. Finding a teacher: look for someone with classical training who plays at or above your goal level. Ask about their approach to technique. A teacher who can demonstrate Rachmaninoff etudes has solved the problems you'll face. Trial lessons (most teachers offer them) let you assess compatibility. You don't need weekly lessons forever - even monthly check-ins with a teacher who can observe and correct would help enormously.

### Human: How do I find a good teacher? I don't know how to evaluate whether someone is actually good or just charges a lot.

### Abstract: Finding a quality piano teacher requires some research. Red flags first: no formal training or unclear background, can't demonstrate repertoire near your goals, only teaches beginners, focuses exclusively on methods books without discussing technique. Green flags: formal training (conservatory, university music program), active performing or has performed at a high level, can explain and demonstrate technique concepts clearly, asks about your goals and tailors instruction, students who have progressed to advanced repertoire. Where to find teachers: local music schools and conservatories often have faculty who teach privately. University music departments sometimes have graduate students teaching - often excellent and affordable. Ask at quality piano stores. Local piano teacher associations have directories. Trial lesson evaluation: do they listen to you play and immediately identify specific issues? Can they demonstrate corrections? Do they explain why, not just what? Do they seem interested in your goals? Trust your instinct about communication style. Price varies widely by market and experience. More expensive doesn't guarantee better, but the cheapest option is often a warning sign. $50-100/hour is common for qualified teachers in many markets; major cities and elite teachers are higher. Ask about their students - where have their students ended up? What repertoire have students successfully learned? Good teachers will talk about this proudly.
