### Human: I'm the chief security officer at a large healthcare system. We're trying to mature our security operations center capabilities, particularly around threat detection and response. What's your perspective on the build versus buy versus MSSP decision?

### Assistant: The build versus buy versus MSSP decision for healthcare SOC capabilities involves several dimensions beyond just cost - effectiveness, speed to capability, talent availability, and regulatory requirements. Framework for the decision: current state assessment: where are you today? If you're starting from minimal SOC capability, the path to mature in-house operations is long and expensive. If you have foundational capabilities and need enhancement, the decision is different. Threat landscape relevance: healthcare faces specific threats - ransomware, PHI exfiltration, medical device attacks - that generic MSSP playbooks may not address well. How critical is healthcare-specific threat intelligence and response to your requirements? Talent reality: experienced SOC analysts are scarce and expensive. Healthcare systems typically can't compete with tech companies on compensation. Building requires not just hiring but retaining and developing analysts in a competitive market. Regulatory considerations: HIPAA requires you to maintain accountability for PHI protection regardless of who operates your SOC. MSSPs need to be structured as business associates with appropriate agreements. Some healthcare systems prefer in-house for control over regulatory compliance. 24/7 operational requirements: true 24/7 SOC coverage requires significant headcount (typically 8-12 analysts minimum for round-the-clock coverage). Many healthcare systems underestimate this requirement. What's your current security operations capability, and what specific capabilities are you trying to mature - detection, response, threat intelligence, or all of the above?

### Human: We have a small internal team of 5 analysts working roughly business hours with on-call after hours. Detection is primarily alert-based from our SIEM, response is ad-hoc. We want to mature to 24/7 coverage with more proactive threat hunting and structured incident response. Our challenge is we can't realistically compete for top security talent against tech companies in our market.

### Abstract: Your situation is common for mid-to-large healthcare systems - you have foundational capability but significant gaps in coverage, proactive detection, and formalized response. The talent constraint is real and should heavily influence your approach. Recommended approach: hybrid model with strategic MSSP partnership for coverage plus internal capability enhancement. Rationale: pure build would take 3-5 years and significant investment with uncertain outcomes given talent constraints. Pure MSSP outsourcing loses institutional knowledge and healthcare context. Hybrid captures benefits of both. Model structure: MSSP for 24/7 monitoring and tier 1 triage: outsource the coverage problem. An MSSP provides round-the-clock monitoring, initial alert triage, and escalation. This addresses your immediate gap without trying to hire 8+ analysts. Select an MSSP with healthcare experience who understands the environment and regulatory requirements. Internal team elevation: your 5 analysts shift from tier 1 alert review to tier 2/3 response and threat hunting. They become the healthcare-specific expertise layer - they understand your environment, clinical workflows, and business context better than any MSSP can. This is more interesting work that aids retention. Incident response: internal team owns incident response with MSSP providing support as needed. Your team knows the environment and can make decisions MSSPs can't. Consider retaining an IR firm for major incidents. Threat hunting: start building this capability internally. Hunting requires deep environmental knowledge. This becomes a differentiating capability for your internal team. SIEM/tooling investment: ensure your SIEM provides visibility the MSSP needs and that your internal team can use for hunting. The hybrid model works best with mature detection capabilities. Critical success factors: clear MSSP SLAs and escalation procedures, well-defined handoff between MSSP triage and internal response, regular tuning of detection rules with MSSP input, and healthcare-specific threat intelligence sharing.

### Human: How should we structure the MSSP evaluation and selection process? What criteria differentiate a capable healthcare-focused MSSP from generic providers?

### Abstract: MSSP selection for healthcare requires evaluating both security capability and healthcare domain expertise. Structured evaluation approach: RFP/RFI process: develop a detailed requirements document covering technical capabilities, healthcare experience, compliance support, and service levels. Require detailed responses, not marketing materials. Include scenario-based questions - how would they handle a specific ransomware incident, how do they tune for medical device networks, etc. Key evaluation criteria: healthcare client base and references: how many healthcare clients of similar size do they currently serve? Request references you can actually call. Healthcare experience shows in operational maturity - understanding of clinical system criticality, HIPAA requirements, and healthcare-specific threats. Generic MSSPs struggle with the context. Analyst qualification and turnover: who will actually monitor your environment? What's their background and training? What's analyst turnover rate? High turnover means constant retraining and lost institutional knowledge. Ask about dedicated versus shared analyst models - some MSSPs assign dedicated analysts to specific clients, which improves context. Healthcare-specific threat intelligence: do they have healthcare-focused threat intel beyond generic feeds? Understanding healthcare-targeted threat actors, TTPs specific to medical environments, and emerging threats to healthcare systems indicates sophistication. Medical device and OT awareness: can they monitor and respond to alerts from clinical systems and medical devices? Many MSSPs treat everything as standard IT. Healthcare environments include OT-like systems with different operational characteristics and criticality. Compliance and audit support: how do they support your HIPAA compliance obligations? Can they provide evidence for audits? Understand their own compliance certifications (SOC 2, HITRUST). Integration capabilities: how well do they integrate with your existing SIEM, EDR, and security stack? Proprietary platform requirements can create lock-in. Technology evaluation: visit their SOC if possible, review their technology stack, understand their automation and orchestration capabilities. Red flags: MSSPs who claim healthcare expertise but can't articulate healthcare-specific threats, those who propose replacing your entire security stack with their platform, and those who can't provide healthcare client references.
