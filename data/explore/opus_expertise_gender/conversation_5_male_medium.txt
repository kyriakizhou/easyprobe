### Human: I've been woodworking as a hobby for a few years, mostly with power tools. I'm getting interested in hand tools but I'm not sure where to start or if it's worth the investment.

### Assistant: Hand tool woodworking is a different discipline that many power tool users find surprisingly rewarding. The benefits are real: quieter work, more control for fine joinery, ability to work in smaller spaces, and for many people, a more meditative process. The main investment is learning to sharpen and tune the tools properly - without that skill, hand tools are frustrating. The tools themselves can range from affordable vintage restoration to expensive premium brands. What kind of woodworking do you do currently, and what draws you to hand tools?

### Human: I build furniture mostly - tables, cabinets, some chairs. I like the idea of not needing to wear hearing protection and being able to work in my basement without worrying about dust as much. I've tried a block plane but couldn't get it to work well.

### Assistant: The block plane struggle is nearly universal for people starting hand tools - it's actually one of the more difficult planes to use well because of its size and typical out-of-box condition. Most planes, even decent ones, need tuning before they work properly: the blade needs to be sharpened to a higher standard than most people expect, the back needs to be flat, and the sole might need lapping. Your first revelation will be how sharp "sharp" actually needs to be for hand tools. The good news: for furniture work, you don't need to abandon power tools entirely. A hybrid approach works great - dimensioning with power tools, joinery and surface prep with hand tools. A well-tuned bench plane for flattening and smoothing, a set of chisels, and a good saw can transform your finishing process. Where to start: get that block plane (or preferably a #4 smoothing plane) properly sharp and tuned - follow Paul Sellers' or Rob Cosman's sharpening tutorials. Once you experience a plane taking whisper-thin shavings, you'll understand what hand tools can do.

### Human: What planes should I actually get? There seem to be endless options and I don't want to buy a bunch of stuff I don't need.

### Abstract: For furniture making, a focused kit beats buying everything. Start with these three: a #4 or #5 bench plane - this is your workhorse for flattening, smoothing, and general work. A #4 is smaller and good for final smoothing; a #5 is more versatile for longer surfaces. Pick one to start. Stanley/Bailey vintage planes from antique stores ($30-75) or modern Woodriver planes ($150-180) are great value. A low-angle block plane - useful for end grain, chamfers, and detail work. Once you learn to tune it, you'll use it constantly. The one you have is fine if you can get it working. A #7 jointer plane (eventually) - for flattening large surfaces and shooting edges. This is where you can spend a lot, but you don't need it immediately. For chisels: a basic set of 1/4", 1/2", 3/4", and 1" covers most work. Narex chisels are excellent value. Avoid the trap of buying specialized planes before you've mastered basics. Many woodworkers accumulate planes they never use. A sharp #4 or #5 with a sharp set of chisels handles 90% of hand tool furniture work.
