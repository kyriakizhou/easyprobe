### Assistant: Alternative data and AI in life insurance underwriting is one of the most significant transformations in the industry, with substantial potential benefits but also meaningful risks that require careful navigation. The regulatory environment is also evolving. Key consideration areas: potential benefits to understand: improved risk selection: alternative data (prescription drug data, motor vehicle records, electronic health records, consumer data) can provide risk signals that traditional underwriting misses or obtains more slowly. Some data sources have shown meaningful predictive power for mortality and morbidity. Process efficiency: accelerated underwriting using alternative data can reduce cycle times from weeks to minutes for many applicants, improving customer experience and conversion rates. Explainability: insurance regulators increasingly require ability to explain underwriting decisions to applicants. Black-box AI models may struggle with this requirement. Some jurisdictions have explicit actuarial justification requirements. Data privacy and consent: using alternative data raises questions about consumer consent and data protection. FCRA requirements, state privacy laws, and HIPAA considerations all apply depending on data source. Regulatory uncertainty: state insurance regulations vary significantly, and guidance on AI/alternative data use is evolving. What may be acceptable today could face regulatory challenge tomorrow. ### Human: On the disparate impact testing - how do we actually do this when we don't collect race data from applicants? We can't directly measure whether our model has different outcomes by race if we don't know applicants' races.

### Abstract: The challenge of testing for disparate impact without direct demographic data is a practical and legal complexity that many insurers face. There are several approaches, each with limitations. Approaches to disparate impact testing without direct demographic data: proxy-based inference methods: Bayesian Improved Surname Geocoding (BISG): uses surname and geographic location (zip code, census tract) to probabilistically estimate race and ethnicity. This is the most common method and is used in fair lending analysis (CFPB, mortgage lending). It's imperfect but provides reasonable population-level estimates. Apply BISG to your applicant pool and analyze outcomes by estimated race/ethnicity. Limitations: BISG is probabilistic - individual estimates may be wrong, though aggregate patterns can be directionally accurate. Less reliable for certain populations (multiracial individuals, less common surnames). Third-party data: some data providers offer inferred demographic attributes based on consumer data aggregation. This raises its own privacy and ethical questions but is used in some fairness testing contexts. Model input analysis: variable correlation: analyze whether model input variables correlate with race proxies. If your model heavily weights variables known to correlate with race (zip code characteristics, credit patterns), that's a warning sign even without outcome testing. Remove or adjust variables with high demographic correlation. Proxy detection: test whether any variable or variable combination effectively serves as a racial proxy. This is more feasible than outcome testing when demographic data isn't available. Synthetic population testing: create synthetic applicant populations with known demographic distributions based on census data. Run your model on these populations and analyze outcome patterns by demographic group. This tests model behavior on representative populations even without real applicant demographic data. External audit: engage third-party firms that specialize in algorithmic fairness testing. They may have access to methods, data, or expertise not available internally. External validation also provides regulatory credibility. Regulatory guidance consideration: some regulators are developing specific guidance on fairness testing in insurance AI. Monitor developments from NAIC and state regulators. Engage constructively with regulators on your approach - proactive engagement can shape expectations and demonstrate good faith. Document your methodology and limitations transparently.
