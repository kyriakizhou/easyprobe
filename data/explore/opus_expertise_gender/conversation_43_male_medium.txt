### Human: I've been doing stand-up comedy for about 2 years at open mics. I'm getting more confident on stage but I want to start getting booked for paid spots. What does it take to make that jump?

### Assistant: The open mic to paid work jump is significant because it requires both comedy chops and understanding the business side. Getting paid means you're providing value to a venue or event beyond "practicing comedian." The typical path: consistent open mic performance building a tight 5-10 minutes, transitioning to showcase spots (unpaid but curated), then earned spots at clubs and eventually paid work. What does your current set look like - how tight is your material, how long can you consistently do well, and have you started getting any showcase opportunities?

### Human: I have a solid 10 minutes that gets consistent laughs. I've done a few showcases and they went well. But I don't really know how to get from here to actually getting paid.

### Abstract: A solid 10 minutes with showcase experience puts you in a reasonable position. The paths to paid work: comedy club spots: clubs book openers/features based on a combination of talent, relationship with the booker, and consistency. You need to be seen by the right people - keep doing showcases at clubs, build relationships with hosts and bookers, ask working comedians how they got booked. Sometimes it's as simple as asking directly after you've proven yourself. Booked shows outside clubs: bar shows, corporate events, private parties, fundraisers - these often pay better than club work at your level. This is relationship-based. Let everyone know you do stand-up. Get on shows produced by other comedians (they're often looking for reliable comics). Produce your own shows: many working comics start by running their own room. You book a bar venue, promote, bring people, and you get stage time while learning production. This builds your reputation and network. Practical steps: develop relationships with other comics on your scene - paid opportunities often flow through networks. Get a promo video of your best set with good audio/video (this is essential for booking). Create basic social media presence with clips. Start asking working comics directly how they get their gigs. What comedy scene are you in - major city, medium market?

### Human: I'm in a medium-sized city - decent scene but not NYC or LA level. There are a few clubs and a lot of bar shows. How do I approach bookers without being annoying?

### Abstract: In a medium market, relationships matter even more because the community is smaller. The booker relationship question: bookers are busy and get approached constantly. The balance: be professional and persistent without being pushy. The approach: first, be consistently good and visible. Bookers often already know who's performing well at open mics and showcases. Being undeniably funny is the best marketing. Making the ask: after you've done a few showcases at a venue and they went well, approach the booker professionally. "Hey, I've really enjoyed performing here. I'd love to be considered for booked shows when you have spots. Here's my info/video if you need it." Then back off. One professional ask is fine; repeated asks become annoying. Following up: if they don't respond or say not yet, keep performing on their open mics, keep getting better, and ask again in 3-6 months. Persistence over time (not persistence in a week) shows commitment. Side door: sometimes hosts or established comics can advocate for you with bookers. If a comedian the booker trusts says "this person is ready," it carries weight. Build those relationships. For medium markets specifically: everyone knows everyone. Being reliable, easy to work with, and promoting shows you're on matters as much as being funny. If you're someone bookers and producers like having around, you'll get more opportunities than a funnier comic who's difficult.
