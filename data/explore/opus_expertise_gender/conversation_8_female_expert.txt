### Human: I'm restructuring our supply chain finance program post-disruption. We relied heavily on reverse factoring with a single large bank and that concentration created problems during liquidity stress. How should I be thinking about diversification versus efficiency in this redesign?

### Assistant: The concentration risk you experienced is classic - reverse factoring efficiency comes from scale, but scale with a single provider creates correlation with their liquidity position exactly when you need the facility most. Redesign considerations: multi-bank facilities with committed capacity from several institutions, dynamic invoice financing platforms that provide market access beyond relationship banks, and consideration of alternative structures (asset-based lending, dynamic discounting with your own balance sheet). What's your supplier base like - concentrated with a few strategic suppliers or diverse?

### Human: Relatively concentrated - top 20 suppliers are 70% of spend. They've been appreciating the early payment program but are sophisticated enough to compare our program terms against alternatives.

### Assistant: Concentrated supplier base actually makes multi-bank reverse factoring manageable - you're not trying to onboard hundreds of suppliers across multiple programs. Design options: establish programs with 2-3 banks with overlapping supplier coverage (suppliers choose where to submit invoices based on pricing), create a primary/backup structure where second bank has committed capacity but only activates in stress, or use a platform intermediary (Taulia, C2FO, PrimeRevenue) that abstracts the funding source from supplier experience. For your sophisticated suppliers, program terms matter - they'll compare your implied financing rate against their own borrowing costs and alternative programs. What pricing power do you have given your spend concentration?

### Human: We're a significant customer for most of them so some leverage, but the large ones have options. We want the program to be genuinely attractive, not just a liquidity extraction mechanism.

### Assistant: That framing is right - the best supply chain finance programs create mutual value, not value transfer. Program design for attractiveness: pricing tied to your credit rating (beneficial for suppliers with weaker credit), transparency about costs and rates, flexibility in participation (suppliers choose which invoices, when), and no mandatory requirements that create dependence. Beyond pricing: consider how the program supports supplier relationship objectives - does it enable longer payment terms that help your working capital while providing early payment at rates suppliers value? Does it provide data visibility that helps their forecasting? Genuinely attractive programs drive adoption organically rather than requiring mandates.

### Human: What about the accounting treatment? Our current structure is off-balance sheet but I've heard there's increased scrutiny on reverse factoring classification.

### Assistant: Accounting scrutiny has increased significantly - FASB and SEC have both signaled attention to supply chain finance disclosure and classification. Key questions: does the bank have recourse to you (explicit or implicit) that makes this effectively borrowing? Are payment terms with suppliers extended specifically to utilize the program? Are there concentration levels with individual banks that create material risk? Current rules require disclosure of outstanding amounts and may require reclassification as debt if substance suggests liability. For your redesign: engage accounting advisors early on structure, ensure terms support trade payable classification, document commercial substance independent of financing, and anticipate enhanced disclosure requirements regardless of classification. Some companies have faced restatement risk; it's not just theoretical.

### Human: How do we structure this operationally? Our treasury team is small and managing multiple bank relationships plus a platform seems complex.

### Assistant: Operational complexity is real. Options for managing it: platform-based approach where a single technology platform (Taulia, Tradeshift, SAP Ariba) manages supplier onboarding, invoice processing, and funding allocation across multiple banks - you interact with one system, platform manages bank relationships. Alternatively, designate one bank as program administrator who coordinates with backup facilities but provides single point of contact. Or build internal capability: supply chain finance is increasingly strategic and may warrant dedicated resource. Minimum viable approach: one primary bank relationship plus one committed backup, with documented procedures for activation. The key is that complexity shouldn't prevent diversification - the COVID and bank stress scenarios demonstrated why single-provider risk is real.
