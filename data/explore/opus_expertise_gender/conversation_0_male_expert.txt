### Human: I'm the CTO of a mid-size fintech company and we're evaluating whether to migrate our core transaction processing system from our on-prem Oracle setup to a distributed architecture on AWS. We process about 2 million transactions daily with strict latency requirements under 50ms for 99th percentile. What's your take on the architectural considerations?

### Assistant: This is a significant migration with real risk given your latency requirements and transaction volume. The architectural considerations span several dimensions. Latency concerns: 50ms p99 is achievable on AWS but requires careful design. Your current Oracle setup likely benefits from memory locality and optimized I/O paths. In a distributed system, you're adding network hops, serialization overhead, and potential coordination latency. Key factors: region and AZ placement (single-AZ for lowest latency trades off against availability), connection pooling and keep-alive strategies, choice of data store (Aurora, DynamoDB, or purpose-built solutions each have different latency profiles). The distributed architecture question: are you considering distributed for scale, availability, or both? At 2M daily transactions (~23 TPS average, but I assume you have significant peak multipliers), you might not need horizontal scale - a well-tuned single-node solution could handle this. The case for distributed is usually fault tolerance and avoiding single points of failure. Data consistency model: financial transactions typically require strong consistency. This constrains your distributed options significantly. You're looking at synchronous replication, which adds latency, or accepting the operational complexity of consensus protocols. What's driving the migration - is it cost, scalability concerns, operational burden of on-prem, or something else? That shapes the architectural direction substantially.

### Human: The drivers are primarily operational - our on-prem infrastructure requires significant dedicated staff, and we're seeing increasing difficulty hiring Oracle DBAs. We're also hitting limits on vertical scaling and our DR strategy is manual and untested. Peak loads are about 10x average, so roughly 230 TPS during market hours.

### Abstract: Those drivers make sense and they point toward specific architectural priorities. For your situation: Aurora PostgreSQL is worth serious consideration for the core transactional workload. You get managed infrastructure (addresses the staffing issue), automated failover and read replicas (addresses DR), and PostgreSQL compatibility means broader talent pool than Oracle. Aurora can handle your throughput - 230 TPS peak is well within its capabilities. Latency-wise, Aurora in provisioned mode with appropriately sized instances can achieve sub-10ms for simple transactions. The migration path from Oracle to PostgreSQL is non-trivial but well-documented. For the 50ms p99 requirement, the architecture needs: application tier in same AZ as primary Aurora instance for critical path, connection pooling (PgBouncer or built into your application framework), prepared statements and optimized queries, and monitoring to catch latency regressions early. DR improvements: Aurora gives you automated backups, point-in-time recovery, and cross-region replication if needed. This is dramatically better than manual DR. But you should define RTO/RPO requirements clearly - they drive whether you need Global Database (more expensive, lower RPO) or standard cross-region replicas. What I'd caution against: don't over-engineer toward microservices or event-sourcing patterns just because you're migrating. If your current monolithic transaction processor works logically, keep that logical structure and focus the migration on the infrastructure layer. Adding architectural complexity during a platform migration multiplies risk.

### Human: We're leaning toward Aurora PostgreSQL as well. The migration from Oracle is the part that concerns me most - we have about 15 years of accumulated PL/SQL logic, complex stored procedures, and Oracle-specific features. How do you approach scoping and de-risking this kind of migration?

### Abstract: 15 years of PL/SQL is a substantial migration challenge. The approach I'd recommend: phase 1 - assessment and scoping (4-6 weeks): inventory everything - stored procedures, triggers, packages, Oracle-specific SQL syntax (CONNECT BY, analytic functions, MERGE statements), data types that don't map cleanly (NUMBER precision, DATE vs TIMESTAMP semantics), and any use of Oracle-specific features (Advanced Queuing, Spatial, Text). AWS Schema Conversion Tool will give you a baseline assessment and flag incompatibilities, but don't trust its "automatic conversion" percentages - they're optimistic. Categorize your PL/SQL into: business logic that must be preserved exactly, code that can be simplified or eliminated, and code that should move to application tier. Often legacy databases accumulate stored procedures that are no longer used or could be application code. Phase 2 - architectural decisions: decide what stays in the database versus moves to application tier. Modern best practice pushes logic to the application for testability and deployability, but migrating 15 years of PL/SQL to application code while also changing platforms is high risk. Pragmatic approach: migrate stored procedures to PostgreSQL PL/pgSQL initially (syntax is similar), then incrementally move logic to application tier post-migration. Phase 3 - parallel running: this is non-negotiable for financial systems. Run both systems simultaneously, comparing outputs transaction by transaction. This catches semantic differences that unit tests miss. Plan for 2-3 months of parallel running minimum. Budget reality: migrations like this typically take 12-18 months and cost more than initial estimates. Build contingency into your planning.
