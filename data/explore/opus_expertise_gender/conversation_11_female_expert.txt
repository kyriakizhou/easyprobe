### Human: I'm working on pricing strategy for a B2B SaaS product moving from seat-based to usage-based pricing. Our customers have expressed interest but internally we're debating how to structure it. What are the key design decisions and pitfalls?

### Assistant: Usage-based pricing is trending but the transition from seat-based requires careful design. Key decisions: what's the value metric (what usage proxy best correlates with customer value?), how do you structure tiers versus linear pricing, how do you handle commitments versus pure consumption, and how do you manage revenue predictability for your own planning. Also critical: how do existing customers migrate without feeling penalized? What usage metric are you considering, and what's your customer size distribution?

### Human: We're considering API calls as the metric - it's measurable and correlates reasonably with value. Customer base ranges from startups using minimal capacity to enterprises with millions of calls monthly. Current seat-based contracts range from $10K to $500K ARR.

### Assistant: API calls are clean to measure but have pitfalls: customers may optimize call patterns in ways that reduce your revenue without reducing their value derived, marginal cost of calls to you is probably near-zero so you need pricing that captures value not cost, and call volume doesn't always correlate with value (some high-value uses are call-efficient). Consider: are there different call types with different value? Should you weight by compute intensity or data volume rather than pure call count? For your size range ($10K-$500K), you likely need: committed capacity tiers for predictable revenue (enterprises want budget certainty), consumption overlay for bursts or growth, and minimum commitments that protect against revenue collapse from efficient customers.

### Human: The minimum commitment question is contentious internally. Sales wants high minimums for revenue protection, customer success argues it creates friction and feels like we're not confident in the value.

### Assistant: Both perspectives are valid and the answer is probably different by customer segment. For enterprise customers ($100K+), committed capacity with consumption overlay is standard and expected - they're doing annual budgeting anyway and want predictability themselves. Minimums here are not friction but alignment. For smaller customers, high minimums feel punitive and create adoption barriers - you want them to start using, grow, and eventually commit. Consider: tiered approach where entry is low/no commitment with consumption pricing, graduating to committed capacity as usage crosses thresholds. This captures small customers who may become large while protecting enterprise revenue. Also: minimize doesn't have to mean cliff - ramp-to-commitment structures give customers time to understand their usage patterns.

### Human: How do we model the revenue impact? We've run scenarios but the uncertainty in usage patterns makes forecasting feel like guessing.

### Assistant: Revenue modeling for usage transitions is inherently uncertain, but you can bound it. Needed: detailed analysis of current customers' likely usage based on their current seat counts, product usage data, and any usage proxies you have, segmented scenario analysis (optimistic/base/pessimistic for high-value versus low-value customers), sensitivity analysis on key assumptions (usage growth rates, commitment levels, churn), and pilot data if possible. The common mistake is modeling "average customer" - but usage distributions are typically highly skewed. Your model needs to capture the tails: what happens if your top 10% of customers optimize aggressively? What if your bottom 50% churn? Run scenarios by customer segment, not just aggregate.

### Human: What about the transition for existing customers? We can't just switch them overnight.

### Assistant: Transition approach matters enormously for retention and trust. Options: grandfathering (existing customers keep old pricing for X years - simple but creates long-term price book complexity), immediate migration with price protection (move to new model but guarantee no price increase for a period - cleaner but requires per-customer analysis), choice architecture (let customers choose between models during transition - useful for uncertain value metric correlation), or renewal migration (transition at contract renewal with advance notice - spreads timing and integrates with natural negotiation point). Whatever you choose: communicate early, model individual customer impact before announcement, arm customer success with talking points and authority to address issues, and be prepared to make exceptions for strategic accounts. Surprising customers with pricing changes destroys trust.
