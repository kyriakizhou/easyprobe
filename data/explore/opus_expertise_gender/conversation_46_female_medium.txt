### Human: I've been fostering children through the county system for about 3 years. We've had four placements and adopted one child. I'd like to support other foster families but I'm not sure how. Are there advocacy or mentorship paths?

### Assistant: What meaningful experience you bring! There are several ways experienced foster parents can support others. Paths include: becoming a licensed foster parent mentor/trainer through your agency, CASA (Court Appointed Special Advocates) volunteer work, advocacy through organizations like FosterClub or state-level groups, support groups for foster parents (facilitating or starting one), and respite care for other foster families. What draws you most - direct support to other foster families, advocacy for systemic change, or supporting foster youth directly?

### Human: Probably direct support to foster families. I remember how overwhelmed we were at the beginning and how much we learned through trial and error. I want to ease that for others.

### Assistant: Your experience with multiple placements plus adoption gives you rare perspective. For mentoring foster families: check if your county agency has a foster parent mentor program - many do, and they actively recruit experienced foster parents. You'd be paired with new families to provide guidance, emotional support, and practical advice. Some states have resource family approval as peer mentors. If no formal program exists, you might propose one to your agency. Informal support also matters: connecting with new foster families through support groups, being available for questions, offering respite care so they get breaks.

### Human: What about the training side? I feel like some of our training before placement didn't prepare us for reality.

### Assistant: That gap is widely recognized! Experienced foster parents often have more practical wisdom than the standard curriculum covers. Options: become certified to co-facilitate PRIDE or MAPP training (the common pre-service trainings) - you'd bring real-world examples to the classroom. Some agencies hire experienced foster parents as "resource family consultants" or trainers. You could also propose developing or delivering specific training on topics you learned the hard way: managing visits with bio parents, trauma-informed parenting in practice, working with the system, or maintaining your own family's wellbeing. Document your knowledge - that preparation makes you more valuable to agencies.

### Human: Are there opportunities that actually pay, or is this all volunteer?

### Assistant: Both exist! Volunteer: mentoring, support groups, CASA, and many advocacy roles. Paid opportunities: agency training positions (often part-time or contract), resource family consultant roles, specialized foster parenting (therapeutic or medical foster care pays more), respite care (some compensation), and nonprofit positions focused on foster care support. Some foster parents create businesses: consulting, speaking, writing, or developing training materials. Your county may not have paid mentor positions, but larger agencies or nonprofits sometimes do. The social work or child welfare nonprofit world has roles for people with lived foster care experience - it's increasingly valued.

### Human: How do I start making connections in this space beyond my own agency?

### Assistant: Expand your network: attend foster parent association meetings and conferences (state and regional), connect with local foster care nonprofits (often host events and need volunteers), join online communities for foster/adoptive parents, engage with advocacy organizations like National Foster Parent Association or state equivalents. Introduce yourself to social workers, GALs, and CASA volunteers you interact with - they know the landscape. When you meet people, be clear about your interest in supporting other families. This space is relationship-driven; your three years of committed fostering plus adoption is significant credibility. People will want to connect you with opportunities.
