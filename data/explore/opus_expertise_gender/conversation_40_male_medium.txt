This knowledge lets you troubleshoot, improvise, and create rather than just follow recipes. There are a few paths: formal culinary principles (technique-focused), food science approach (understanding the chemistry), or specific cuisine deep-dives (mastering one tradition's logic). ### Human: I'm most interested in understanding why things work - like why certain techniques produce certain results. I read Serious Eats a lot but I want to go deeper. I feel like I know how to cook but not how to think about cooking.

### Abstract: You're describing the transition from recipe-follower to cook. The resources you want: "The Food Lab" by Kenji López-Alt (if you haven't already) - it's Serious Eats in book form but more comprehensive, covering why techniques work through tested experiments. "Salt Fat Acid Heat" by Samin Nosrat - focuses on the four fundamental elements and how to think about balancing them. Great for intuition-building. "On Food and Cooking" by Harold McGee - the food science bible. Dense and reference-oriented, not a cover-to-cover read. When you want to understand why something works at a molecular level, this is where you look. "Cooking for Geeks" by Jeff Potter - more accessible science approach with experiments you can try. Practical application: don't just read - cook deliberately. When you learn "maillard reaction requires high heat and dry surface," test it: cook two steaks, one with dried surface, one wet. Taste the difference. This builds embodied understanding, not just knowledge. The skill you're developing is hypothesis-testing: "If I do X, I expect Y because of Z." Then test it. When you're wrong, figure out why. This is how you develop cooking intuition grounded in understanding. What specific techniques or results are you trying to master or understand better?

### Human: Sauces are my weakness - I can follow a recipe but I don't really understand how to build them from scratch or fix them when they go wrong. What should I understand about sauces?

### Abstract: Sauces are a great focus because they illustrate core principles. The fundamental categories: emulsions (fat dispersed in water or vice versa) - mayonnaise, vinaigrettes, hollandaise, butter sauces. The science: getting and keeping two things mixed that don't want to mix. Emulsifiers (egg yolk, mustard) help. Temperature, ratio, and agitation rate matter. When they break, you understand why and can fix them. Reductions - flavor concentration through evaporation. Stock, wine, pan drippings reduced to intensify flavor. The concept: you're evaporating water to concentrate everything else. You can always add more liquid back. Starch-thickened - roux-based sauces (béchamel, velouté), slurries. The science: starch granules absorb water and swell when heated. Understanding this helps you know why you cook roux longer or shorter (flavor development versus thickening power). Technique knowledge to build: mother sauce framework (béchamel, velouté, espagnole, tomato, hollandaise) - not because you'll make classic French sauces, but because they're templates that teach proportion and technique. Pan sauce technique - deglaze, add liquid, reduce, mount with butter. This is infinitely adaptable once you understand the steps. Emulsion technique - how to start, maintain, and rescue emulsions. Practice: make mayonnaise from scratch repeatedly until it's automatic. Make a pan sauce every time you sear meat. Make béchamel and turn it into cheese sauce, then into soufflé base. The repetition builds intuition.
