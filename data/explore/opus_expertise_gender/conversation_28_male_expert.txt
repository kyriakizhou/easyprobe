### Human: I'm a senior producer at a AAA game studio. We're struggling with crunch culture - long hours, burnout, turnover. Leadership wants to address it but we keep falling into the same patterns with each release. How do we actually change this?

### Assistant: Crunch culture in game development is a structural problem that can't be solved with individual good intentions or periodic initiatives. It requires fundamental changes to how projects are scoped, scheduled, and managed. The pattern persists because the underlying causes aren't addressed. Understanding why crunch recurs: feature creep and scope uncertainty: games involve significant creative uncertainty. Features look different in execution than conception. This leads to constant scope changes, which scheduling doesn't accommodate. The slack gets absorbed by crunch. Unrealistic scheduling: schedules often assume everything goes right - no tech problems, no iteration, no discovery work. When reality diverges (it always does), crunch absorbs the difference. Milestone-driven death marches: external commitments (publisher milestones, marketing windows, platform certification deadlines) create hard deadlines that don't flex. When scope and deadlines conflict, people flex instead. Cultural normalization: crunch becomes expected, planned for, and even romanticized. Teams don't push back because it's "how game development works." New hires see it as industry norm. False productivity calculation: leadership may believe crunch is actually productive (it often isn't - productivity per hour drops, bugs increase, people burn out and leave, taking knowledge with them). Without measuring true productivity, the calculus appears to favor crunch. What's your current production structure - waterfall, agile, or some hybrid? And where in the development cycle does crunch typically manifest most severely?

### Human: We use a hybrid approach - vertical slices with milestones, then more agile iteration in production. Crunch tends to ramp up significantly in the last 6-9 months before ship, and then again for post-launch patches. The schedule starts reasonable but erodes as we discover issues or need to iterate on features that aren't working.

### Abstract: The pattern you describe - reasonable start, progressive erosion, severe end-of-project crunch - is the most common failure mode. The structure of vertical slice + agile iteration is sound in theory but often fails in practice because the buffer for discovery and iteration gets consumed. Structural changes to address this: scope management discipline: the core issue is scope versus schedule. When you discover a feature isn't working, something must give - either the feature is cut/descoped, the schedule extends, or people crunch. Currently, people crunch. Create explicit decision frameworks for scope changes: what's the cost (in effort, in schedule) and who approves it? Make the tradeoffs visible rather than absorbing them in crunch. Practice saying "no" or "not this release." Buffer institutionalization: bake buffer into schedules explicitly and protect it. Not hidden buffer that gets negotiated away, but explicit contingency that leadership understands and defends. Industry estimates suggest 20-30% buffer is appropriate for game development given inherent uncertainty. Milestone decoupling: if milestone deadlines are driving crunch, examine what's actually required at each milestone. Can you negotiate smaller scope commitments with publishers while maintaining schedule? Can you build more slack between milestones? The alternative is building in crunch by committing to unachievable scope. Hours tracking and limits: make working hours visible. Track overtime explicitly. Set limits (even if initially high) and treat exceeding them as a problem to be solved, not a heroic effort to be praised. Visibility creates accountability. Post-mortems that drive change: after each release, conduct honest retrospectives on crunch. Identify specific decisions or moments where crunch became inevitable. Use these to change future behavior, not just document problems. If post-mortems don't lead to changes, they're theater. Long-term velocity tracking: measure actual team productivity over time. Show leadership that crunch-driven periods often produce less net output than sustainable periods due to bugs, rework, and turnover replacement costs.

### Human: We do post-mortems but the lessons rarely stick to the next project. A new project starts with good intentions, then pressure builds and we fall into old patterns. How do we make the changes actually persist?

### Abstract: The failure of post-mortem insights to persist across projects is a common organizational learning failure. Knowledge exists but doesn't translate to changed behavior. This requires structural and cultural interventions. Making change persistent: institutionalize in process, not just memory: post-mortem insights that remain as documents or tribal knowledge will be forgotten under pressure. Convert insights into formal process changes: scheduling templates with mandatory buffers, approval workflows for scope changes, working hours policies with enforcement mechanisms. The lesson "we should have cut scope earlier" becomes "scope changes after X milestone require executive sign-off with explicit schedule impact analysis." Leadership accountability structure: assign specific accountability for sustainable production practices to a leader with authority. This person owns working hours monitoring, production health metrics, and has the authority to intervene when patterns emerge. Without accountability, good intentions diffuse. Early warning systems: the time to prevent crunch is early in the project, not when you're 6 months from ship and behind schedule. Create metrics and checkpoints that identify trajectory problems early: schedule versus actual tracking, team health surveys, velocity trends. When indicators show problems, intervene before it's too late. Cultural change: hire and promote leaders who model sustainable practices. It only takes a few leaders who romanticize crunch to undermine broader change. Conversely, leaders who refuse to crunch and deliver successfully demonstrate alternatives. This is slow but essential. Cross-project continuity: if different projects have different production leadership, lessons don't transfer. Create mechanisms for cross-project learning - production communities of practice, shared retrospective databases, explicit knowledge transfer between projects. External accountability: some studios have found that external commitments (to employees, to publishers, even publicly stated values) create accountability that internal intentions don't. If you've told employees you're committed to sustainable development, you've created pressure to follow through. Patience and persistence: culture change takes years, not months. Accept that you'll have setbacks and partial progress. Maintain commitment through leadership transitions and project pressures. The alternative is continued cycles of intention and failure.
