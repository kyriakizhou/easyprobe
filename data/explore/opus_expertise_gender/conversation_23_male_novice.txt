### Human: My boss just told me I need to create a budget for my department but I've never done anything like that. I don't even know where to start.

### Abstract: Creating your first department budget can feel overwhelming, but it's really about organizing information you probably already know into a standard format. Let's break it down. First, some questions: what kind of department do you manage (sales, marketing, operations, etc.)? Does your company have a template or format you need to follow? And is this for the upcoming year, quarter, or something else? Knowing these helps me give you specific guidance.

### Human: I manage a small marketing team - 4 people. There's no template, boss just said to figure out what we need for next year. We've never had a formal budget before.

### Abstract: Okay, starting from scratch is actually fine - you get to build something that makes sense for your team. Here's the basic approach: list all your expenses by category. For marketing, typical categories are: salaries and benefits (your team's compensation), software and tools (marketing automation, design tools, analytics), advertising spend (paid media, campaigns), contractor or agency fees, events and tradeshows, miscellaneous (training, supplies). Pull last year's actual spending for each category if you can - check with finance or your corporate card statements. Then project next year: same as this year, or do you need more for a new initiative? Less because you're cutting something? Add 5-10% buffer for unexpected costs. Put it all in a spreadsheet with monthly or quarterly breakdowns. Want me to walk through how to estimate any specific category?

### Human: The salaries part confuses me. Do I include benefits? And what about raises?

### Abstract: Good questions - salary budgeting has a few components. Yes, include benefits - typically budget "fully loaded" cost, not just base salary. Your HR or finance team can give you the exact multiplier, but benefits (health insurance, 401k match, payroll taxes) typically add 25-40% on top of base salary. So a $60,000 salary might cost $75,000-84,000 fully loaded. For raises: budget for expected annual increases. If your company typically gives 3-4% raises, build that in starting from the expected raise date (often anniversary or January). If you're expecting any promotions or new hires, include those at the timing you expect them. Here's the formula per person: current salary + expected raise + benefits multiplier = total cost. Then add them up for your team total. Check with HR about the exact benefits multiplier your company uses - using the real number shows you did your homework. Don't forget any open positions you're planning to fill.
