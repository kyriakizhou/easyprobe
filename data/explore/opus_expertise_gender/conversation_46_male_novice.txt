### Human: How do I actually make good coffee at home? Everything I make tastes watery or bitter.

### Abstract: Those two problems - watery and bitter - are actually opposite ends of the extraction spectrum, which means small adjustments can fix both. Coffee is basically just water extracting flavor compounds from ground beans. Under-extracted = weak and watery. Over-extracted = bitter and harsh. The variables that control this are: coffee-to-water ratio, grind size, water temperature, and brew time. Can you tell me what you're using to make coffee right now? Drip machine, French press, pour-over, instant?

### Human: Just a basic Mr. Coffee drip machine. I use the pre-ground stuff from the grocery store.

### Abstract: That's a good starting point - drip machines are pretty forgiving once you nail the basics. Most common mistakes: not using enough coffee (watery taste) or using water that's not hot enough (weak extraction). Here's the fix: use more coffee than you think - a good ratio is about 2 tablespoons per 6oz cup of water, or roughly 1g of coffee per 15-17g of water if you want to be precise. Most people under-dose significantly. Make sure your machine is actually heating water properly - if it's old, it might not be getting hot enough (should be 195-205°F). Clean your machine - mineral buildup and old oils make coffee taste off. Run vinegar and water through it, then plain water a few times. If you're still getting watery coffee with enough grounds, the pre-ground might be the issue - it goes stale quickly. Fresh-ground coffee makes a noticeable difference.

### Human: Is getting a grinder worth it? That seems like a lot of extra work.

### Abstract: Honestly, yes - a grinder is the single biggest upgrade you can make for coffee quality. Pre-ground coffee starts going stale within days of being ground because more surface area is exposed to air. Whole beans stay fresh much longer. The difference is immediately noticeable. That said, it does add a step. Easiest option: a simple burr grinder (not blade) takes 20 seconds to grind enough for a pot. Good entry-level burr grinders run $30-50 (Bodum Bistro, JavaPresse manual grinder). Blade grinders are cheaper ($15-20) and work okay but produce inconsistent particle size. Beyond fresher coffee, grinding yourself lets you buy much better beans - local roasters, specialty brands - that only sell whole bean. You don't have to grind your own, but if you want noticeably better coffee and are willing to spend $40 and add 30 seconds to your routine, it's worth it. Start with everything else first (ratio, machine cleanliness) and upgrade to grinding later if you want to level up further.
