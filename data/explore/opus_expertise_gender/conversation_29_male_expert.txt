### Human: I'm a founding partner at a growth equity firm. We're seeing AI-native companies with impressive demos and rapid growth, but I'm struggling to assess sustainable competitive advantage. Traditional moats seem less clear when foundation models are commoditizing. How are you thinking about this?

### Assistant: AI-native company moats are genuinely different from traditional software moats, and much of the current investment in the space may not generate durable returns as the stack commoditizes. Healthy skepticism about sustainable advantage is warranted. The commoditization risk: foundation model access is rapidly becoming commoditized. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta (open source), and others provide increasingly capable models via API. A company whose primary value is "GPT wrapper with a nice UI" faces existential risk as base model capabilities improve and larger platforms add similar features. The moat question: given that, what creates durable advantage? Data moats: proprietary data that improves model performance or enables fine-tuning that competitors can't replicate. This requires data that is: unique to the company (not publicly available), generated through the product (network effects), and genuinely differentiating for model quality. Many claimed data moats are weaker than presented - fine-tuning on customer data may provide limited advantage over base model improvements. Workflow integration: deep integration into customer workflows creates switching costs. If the AI is embedded in how customers work, not just a point solution, displacement is harder. Look for products where the AI learns from and adapts to specific customer processes. This is closer to traditional enterprise software moats. Network effects: rare in AI but powerful when present. Products where more users genuinely improve the product for all users (not just provide more data) can build sustainable advantage. Vertical specialization: AI applied to domains with specialized knowledge requirements, regulatory complexity, or industry-specific workflows may sustain advantage longer than horizontal applications. What vertical or use case are you most focused on?

### Human: We're seeing a lot of deal flow in AI for enterprise workflows - customer service, sales enablement, code generation, content creation. These seem to be the highest traction areas. But I'm concerned about how sustainable any of this is - Microsoft, Google, and Salesforce all have AI initiatives in these exact spaces.

### Abstract: Your concern about platform competition in those categories is well-founded. Let me assess each: customer service: high platform risk. Zendesk, Salesforce, Intercom, and others are all building AI into their existing platforms. Standalone AI customer service companies need to either integrate with and be better than the native offerings (hard to sustain) or replace the platforms entirely (requires going beyond AI differentiation). First-mover advantage is limited because incumbents have the customer data and workflow integration. Sales enablement: similar dynamics. Salesforce, HubSpot, and Outreach are all adding AI. Standalone AI sales tools need to be significantly better AND integrate with CRM platforms that may prioritize their native solutions. Watch for platform bundling dynamics that disadvantage standalone vendors. Code generation: GitHub Copilot has significant market position with Microsoft distribution. Competing requires being substantially better or serving segments GitHub doesn't prioritize. Enterprise-specific needs (security, compliance, codebase-specific fine-tuning) may provide differentiation, but Microsoft can address these too. Content creation: possibly the most sustainable of these categories because content workflows are more fragmented. Adobe, Google, Microsoft are all active, but the market is broad enough for specialized tools. Still, sustainable advantage requires more than base model capabilities. Investment framework for these categories: if platform competition is inevitable, the investment question becomes: can this company build enough value before platform competition arrives? Can they achieve scale, customer lock-in, or capability differentiation that survives platform entry? Can they potentially be acquired by a platform (exit rather than sustained competition)? Position sizing and valuation discipline matter enormously in high-uncertainty categories.

### Human: How do you actually diligence the claimed data and technology moats? Companies always claim their model is better and their data is unique, but verifying these claims is challenging.

### Abstract: Claimed AI moats are often exaggerated or misunderstood, and the verification challenge is real. Most investors lack the technical depth to independently assess claims, creating information asymmetry that advantages companies with good narratives over those with real advantages. Diligence approaches: technical deep-dives with real expertise: don't rely on generalist investors to assess AI claims. Engage technical advisors who work in the relevant ML domain - ideally practitioners, not just academics. Have them evaluate: is the claimed technical approach sound? Is the data advantage real? How easily could this be replicated? What would it cost to rebuild? Customer-validated performance: the best evidence for model superiority is customer adoption and retention based on demonstrated performance. Diligence should include extensive customer calls focused on: why did you choose this over alternatives? Have you tested alternatives? What would make you switch? Can you quantify the performance difference? Skepticism toward demo performance is warranted - production reality often differs. Benchmark evaluation: for claims about model quality, request benchmark performance on industry-standard evaluations. If the company says their model is better, they should be able to demonstrate it on relevant benchmarks. Be wary of cherry-picked or self-constructed benchmarks that favor the company's approach. Data source and uniqueness analysis: for data moat claims, understand: where does the data come from? Is it actually proprietary, or is it data that others could access? How much data do they have versus competitors? Is the data sufficiently fresh and relevant? How is the data flywheel supposed to work as the product scales? Technical diligence on fine-tuning and training: understand what they're actually doing with data. Is it true fine-tuning that requires significant data and compute, or is it prompt engineering and RAG that provides less durable advantage? The technical sophistication of the approach matters for defensibility. Competitive intelligence: talk to competitors, former employees (appropriately), and industry experts about relative positioning. Companies exist in competitive context, not isolation. Red flags: companies that resist technical diligence or can't articulate their technical differentiation clearly are concerning.
