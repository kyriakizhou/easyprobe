### Human: I'm a chief medical officer at a mid-size pharma company. We're trying to optimize our clinical development strategy for a novel oncology compound - specifically around adaptive trial designs. What's your perspective on when adaptive designs are most valuable versus traditional fixed designs?

### Assistant: Adaptive designs can offer significant advantages but aren't universally superior to fixed designs. The decision depends on the specific program characteristics and organizational capabilities. Where adaptive designs provide most value: uncertainty in dose-response: if you're uncertain about optimal dose or schedule, adaptive designs that allow dose modification based on interim data can avoid committing to suboptimal dosing early. This is common in oncology where therapeutic windows vary across tumor types and patient populations. Patient population heterogeneity: if your compound may work better in specific biomarker-defined subgroups but you're uncertain which, adaptive enrichment designs allow you to modify enrollment based on interim efficacy signals. Platform trials: if you're testing multiple arms or combinations, adaptive platform designs allow dropping ineffective arms and adding new ones. Resource efficiency in competitive landscape: adaptive designs can reach decisions faster with smaller total enrollment if treatments have clear signals - either positive or negative. This matters in competitive oncology where speed matters. Where fixed designs may be preferable: regulatory pathway certainty: adaptive designs require more complex regulatory interaction and pre-specification. If you have high confidence in your dose, population, and endpoint, a clean fixed design may be faster to agree with FDA/EMA and simpler to execute. Operational complexity: adaptive designs require real-time data analysis, potentially unblinded data review, and rapid operational pivots. If your organization lacks experience with adaptive execution, operational failure risk increases. What's the specific compound and indication, and what's the key uncertainty you're trying to address?

### Human: It's a novel targeted therapy for a specific genomic alteration that occurs across multiple tumor types. We're most uncertain about the relative efficacy across tumor types and whether to pursue a tumor-agnostic indication or focus on specific cancers. Our Phase 1 data is promising but we have limited sample sizes in any single tumor type.

### Abstract: For a tumor-agnostic indication strategy with a genomic-selected population, adaptive design is particularly well-suited. The specific uncertainties you describe - differential efficacy across tumor types with limited data in any single type - are exactly what adaptive approaches address. Recommended approach: basket trial with adaptive enrichment. Design structure: enroll patients across tumor types with your target genomic alteration. Implement pre-specified interim analyses that evaluate response rates by tumor type. Based on interim data, adaptively modify enrollment - drop tumor types showing minimal activity, expand enrollment in promising tumor types, potentially add new tumor types based on mechanistic rationale. Statistical framework: Bayesian hierarchical modeling works well for basket trials. It allows borrowing of information across tumor types (the genomic mechanism should confer some similarity) while allowing for heterogeneity. This increases statistical efficiency when signals are consistent and identifies outliers when they exist. Pre-specify rules for "graduation" of tumor types (sufficient evidence of efficacy to pursue registration), "dropping" of tumor types (futility), and continued enrollment (promising but uncertain). Regulatory considerations for tumor-agnostic path: FDA's tumor-agnostic approval pathway (precedents: Keytruda for MSI-high, larotrectinib for NTRK fusions) requires demonstrating efficacy across tumor types with a consistent response rate. Your adaptive basket design should be discussed with FDA early - Breakthrough Therapy designation if warranted gives you more interaction. Key regulatory question: will FDA accept the adaptive design for registration, or will they require confirmatory fixed-design studies in specific tumor types? Practical considerations: ensure your biomarker assay strategy supports the trial - companion diagnostic development needs to parallel clinical development. Genomic alteration prevalence across tumor types affects enrollment feasibility.

### Human: We're planning to request a Type B meeting with FDA to discuss the registration strategy. What should we be preparing to maximize the value of that interaction and avoid common pitfalls?

### Abstract: Type B meetings for novel registration strategies require careful preparation. The goal is to get clear FDA feedback on your proposed path while demonstrating scientific rigor and regulatory awareness. Pre-meeting preparation: briefing document is critical: this is your primary communication vehicle. Structure it to present your proposal clearly, anticipate FDA questions, and provide supporting rationale. Key sections: executive summary with your specific questions, compound and mechanism overview, clinical data summary (Phase 1 results by tumor type), proposed registration strategy with adaptive trial design, biomarker and companion diagnostic strategy, and statistical analysis plan outline. The document should be detailed enough that reviewers can evaluate your proposal without the meeting. Formulate specific questions: avoid vague questions like "Does FDA agree with our approach?" Instead: "Does FDA consider the proposed primary endpoint (ORR per BICR) appropriate for the proposed tumor-agnostic indication?" "Is the proposed sample size and response rate threshold in each tumor type acceptable for registration?" "Will FDA accept interim analysis-based adaptive enrichment within the registration trial?" FDA wants to answer concrete questions. Anticipate FDA concerns: for tumor-agnostic adaptive designs, expect questions on: statistical validity of adaptive enrichment (how do you control type I error?), consistency of treatment effect across tumor types (what heterogeneity is acceptable?), biomarker assay validation and cutoff selection, comparator arm necessity (single-arm vs randomized), durability of response requirements. Have positions and supporting data ready. Common pitfalls to avoid: over-presenting clinical data: reviewers have read the briefing document. Don't spend meeting time rehashing data. Focus on discussion of your questions and FDA concerns. Not bringing the right people: include your clinical lead, regulatory lead, biostatistician (critical for adaptive design discussions), and CMC/biomarker representative if CDx discussions are anticipated. Being defensive: if FDA pushes back on elements of your proposal, listen and understand their reasoning. The goal is to find a path that works, not to win an argument.
