### Human: I'm leading digital transformation at a large regional health system - about 15 hospitals and 200 clinics. We're mid-implementation on a unified EHR but realizing that the technology is the easy part. The workflow change management is where we're struggling. What frameworks are you seeing work?

### Assistant: You've identified the central challenge - EHR implementations fail or underperform on adoption and workflow, not technology. The scale of your system (15 hospitals, 200 clinics) adds complexity because workflows vary legitimately across sites, specialties, and care settings. Successful approaches I've seen: identify physician and nursing champions at each site who drive peer adoption rather than relying on top-down mandates, design for workflow variability rather than forcing standardization where it doesn't add value, and sequence rollout to build internal expertise before scaling. What's your current implementation approach and what specific resistance are you encountering?

### Human: We've been doing a phased rollout by region. Resistance is coming from experienced clinicians who say the new workflows are slower and designed by people who don't understand patient care. They're not entirely wrong - some standardized workflows don't fit their specialties.

### Assistant: The experienced clinician resistance is particularly important to address because they're often informal leaders whose adoption signals matter to peers. Their feedback about workflow fit is almost certainly accurate in some cases - standardized EHR workflows are designed for modal cases and often miss specialty-specific needs. Approaches: establish specialty-specific workgroups that can customize within guardrails, implement "workflow liaisons" who are clinicians (not just IT) working directly with resistant sites, separate what must be standardized (safety, interoperability, billing requirements) from what can be locally optimized. Also: measure and address the "slower" complaint - is it temporary learning curve or permanent workflow degradation? Both are real but require different responses.

### Human: The speed issue is complex. Training says 6-week adaptation period, but our data shows productivity hasn't recovered at sites that went live 6 months ago. Clinicians are building workarounds.

### Assistant: If productivity hasn't recovered at 6 months, you have a design problem, not a training problem. The workarounds are signals - they reveal where the system doesn't serve clinical workflow. This requires: systematic workaround documentation (what are people doing instead, why?), root cause analysis (is it configuration, training, or fundamental workflow mismatch?), and rapid iteration on the problematic workflows. Six months of productivity loss across your system has massive operational and financial implications. Are you tracking this granularly enough to identify which workflows are problematic versus which sites have adoption issues? The interventions are different.

### Human: We track at the site level but not workflow level. We're realizing our metrics framework wasn't designed for this kind of analysis.

### Assistant: Common gap. Building workflow-level visibility now is essential for both remediation and future optimization. Needed: EHR usage analytics (where do clinicians spend time, where do they deviate from designed workflow, where are error rates high?), clinical observation studies in problematic areas (trained observers watching actual work), and time-motion analysis for key workflows. The EHR should be generating data you can analyze - work with your vendor on analytics capabilities. Many systems underutilize the data their EHR produces. This diagnostic investment pays off in targeted intervention rather than broad-based re-training that may miss the actual problems. It also gives you defensible data for conversations with skeptical clinicians.

### Human: How do we rebuild trust with the clinical staff who feel we've damaged their practice? This isn't just a technical problem anymore.

### Assistant: Trust rebuilding requires: acknowledging the problems openly (clinicians know their experience, denying it destroys credibility), demonstrating responsiveness through visible changes based on their feedback, involving clinical staff in solution design not just feedback gathering, and celebrating wins when workflow improvements succeed. The communication shift is from "here's what you need to adopt" to "help us fix this together." Consider executive rounding with clinical staff specifically about EHR experience, visible commitment from CMO and CNO (this is their system now, not an IT project), and rapid-cycle improvement with short feedback loops. Trust recovery takes longer than trust destruction, but it's possible if you show sustained commitment to clinical partnership rather than compliance-based implementation.
