### Human: I'm advising a sovereign wealth fund on their approach to direct private equity co-investment. They want to reduce fee drag from fund investments by building co-investment capability, but lack the infrastructure and expertise to source and evaluate deals independently. What's the right architecture?

### Assistant: Sovereign wealth fund co-investment programs are increasingly common for fee optimization, but the capability requirements are often underestimated. The spectrum runs from passive co-investment (accepting allocations offered by GP partners with limited diligence) to active co-investment (independent sourcing, deep diligence, potentially leading deals). Passive requires less infrastructure but provides less fee benefit and may result in adverse selection - GPs may offer co-investment on deals where they want to reduce their own exposure. Active provides better economics and deal selection but requires substantial team and process investment. Most sovereigns evolve through stages: start with passive co-invest alongside trusted GPs, build capability over time, eventually develop some independent sourcing. What's your current fund portfolio and GP relationship depth?

### Human: We have commitments to about 40 PE funds across buyout, growth, and infrastructure. Relationships vary - some GPs we've been with for 15 years, others are newer. We've done opportunistic co-investment but nothing systematic.

### Assistant: Forty GP relationships with depth variation provides a foundation but not sufficient deal flow for a meaningful co-investment program. The math: if 30% of your GPs offer co-investment opportunities and you see 2-3 per GP per year, you're seeing maybe 25-35 opportunities annually. With selectivity (you should reject many), you might do 8-12 co-investments per year. That's meaningful but not transformative. Architecture recommendations: formalize co-investment agreements with your deepest GP relationships - priority allocation rights, notice periods, fee arrangements should be explicit in side letters. Build a lean internal team (3-5 people initially) with transaction execution capability and sector expertise aligned to your fund portfolio. Develop rapid diligence processes - GP-offered co-investment typically has compressed timelines. Establish governance that enables quick decisions without compromising rigor. For the longer term: as capability builds, expand beyond GP-offered deals to introduce independent sourcing through investment banks, advisors, and direct company relationships. What's your target allocation to co-investment relative to fund commitments?

### Human: Target is to deploy 30% of private equity capital through co-investment within five years. That seems aggressive given our current capabilities.

### Assistant: 30% co-investment allocation in five years is aggressive but achievable with deliberate capability building. The challenge is ramping deployment while building team and processes - you can't fully develop capability without deploying capital, but deploying without capability creates risk. Phased approach: Year 1-2, target 10-15% co-investment, focused on GP-offered deals alongside your highest-conviction managers. Use this period to build team, refine processes, and develop sector expertise. Year 3-4, expand to 20-25%, begin supplementing GP deal flow with independent sourcing in sectors where you've built expertise. Year 5, reach 30% with mature capability across sourcing, diligence, and portfolio management. Critical success factors: hire experienced private equity professionals who can evaluate deals independently, not just process GP-provided materials; build sector expertise that allows you to move quickly on relevant opportunities; establish governance that permits decisions within GP-offered timelines (often 2-3 weeks); and maintain GP relationships so that deal flow continues even as you become more selective. What's your current investment committee structure and decision timeline?

### Human: Investment committee meets monthly and requires extensive materials. A 2-3 week decision timeline for co-investment is not achievable with current governance.

### Assistant: Monthly IC with extensive materials is fundamentally incompatible with co-investment timelines. GPs offer co-investment when deals are signing, not months in advance. Governance redesign options: delegate co-investment authority to a subset of IC members who can convene on short notice (co-investment committee with emergency meeting capability); establish pre-approval parameters where deals meeting certain criteria (GP relationship quality, sector, size, leverage) can proceed with expedited approval; or grant executive authority to CIO or designated team for co-investments below a threshold size with IC ratification. The key principle: maintain rigor and accountability without requiring full committee deliberation on GP timelines. Many sovereigns and large institutions have separate governance tracks for co-investment versus fund commitments - fund commitments involve strategy and manager selection (appropriate for monthly IC), co-investments involve transaction execution on already-vetted managers (appropriate for expedited process). The change management required is significant - boards and ICs accustomed to full deliberation may resist delegation. Frame it as necessary infrastructure for the fee savings objective. What's your board's appetite for governance evolution?

### Human: The board is generally supportive of co-investment economics but risk-averse about changing governance. They've had negative experiences with delegated authority in other contexts.

### Assistant: Risk-averse boards with negative delegation experiences require governance solutions that provide comfort while enabling speed. Approaches: constrained delegation with explicit guardrails - pre-defined parameters (maximum check size, required GP relationship tenure, sector limits, leverage caps) outside of which expedited approval doesn't apply. If a deal falls outside parameters, full IC process applies. Retrospective accountability: all co-investments made under delegated authority reviewed at next monthly IC with full documentation; pattern of poor decisions triggers authority revision. Staged delegation: start with very narrow authority (only Tier 1 GPs, only sectors with internal expertise, smaller check sizes) and expand as track record builds. Co-investment committee composition that includes board representation - a board member on the expedited approval committee provides direct oversight without requiring full board convening. The negative experiences in other contexts may not be applicable to co-investment, which has structural protections (you're co-investing alongside sophisticated GPs who've done extensive diligence). Help the board distinguish between delegation that removes oversight and delegation that adapts oversight to transaction requirements. What were the negative experiences with delegation, if you can share generally?
