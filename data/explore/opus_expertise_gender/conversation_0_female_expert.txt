### Human: I'm refactoring our event-driven microservices architecture and reconsidering our approach to saga orchestration versus choreography. Currently using choreography but we're hitting issues with distributed tracing and understanding failure modes across complex workflows. What's your take on the tradeoffs at scale?

### Assistant: Both patterns have legitimate use cases, and the tradeoffs become more pronounced at scale. Choreography's decentralization gives you loose coupling and independent deployability, but as you've discovered, observability and failure reasoning become genuinely hard. With saga choreography, you need correlation IDs propagating through every event, centralized log aggregation, and often custom tooling to reconstruct workflow state across services. Orchestration centralizes that logic at the cost of creating a coordinator that becomes a coupling point. What's your current service count and typical saga complexity?

### Human: About 40 services, some sagas span 8-10 services with compensating transactions. Our SRE team spends significant time tracing failures through Kafka topic logs.

### Assistant: At that complexity, hybrid approaches often work better than pure patterns. Consider: orchestration for complex multi-step workflows where reasoning about state matters (order fulfillment, payment processing), choreography for simpler reactive flows where services truly operate independently. The 8-10 service sagas are prime candidates for orchestration - tools like Temporal or Camunda handle the state machine, retries, and compensation logic while providing built-in observability. You get a single place to see saga state rather than reconstructing it from distributed events.

### Human: We've looked at Temporal. Concerns are: adding another infrastructure dependency, and whether it becomes a single point of failure. How do production deployments handle this?

### Assistant: Valid concerns. Temporal is designed for high availability - you run multiple history and matching services behind a database (Cassandra or MySQL). It's stateless at the service level; state lives in the database. Failure modes are well-understood: workers can restart and pick up where they left off, the server cluster handles failover. The infrastructure dependency is real but arguably less than the hidden complexity of managing distributed saga state yourself. Production deployments typically: run Temporal in its own reliability tier with aggressive monitoring, implement circuit breakers between Temporal and business services, and design workflows to be idempotent and resumable. Netflix, Uber, and others run it at significant scale.

### Human: What about the workflow definition coupling? Services need to know about the orchestrator and implement activity interfaces. That's tighter coupling than pure events.

### Assistant: True, and it's the fundamental tradeoff. With orchestration, services are coupled to activity contracts rather than event schemas - different coupling, not necessarily more. The question is which coupling serves you better. Activity-based coupling is explicit and version-managed; you know exactly who calls what. Event choreography coupling is implicit and can lead to undocumented dependencies. For services in your critical saga paths, that explicit coupling may be appropriate. Services that truly should remain decoupled can still subscribe to events emitted by the orchestrated workflow. The architecture doesn't have to be uniformly one pattern - most mature systems mix based on context.

### Human: Fair point. Any recommendations on migration strategy? We can't rewrite everything at once.

### Assistant: Strangler pattern works well here. Identify your highest-pain sagas first - likely the 8-10 service ones your SRE team struggles with. Implement those in Temporal while keeping existing services largely unchanged; activities often wrap existing service calls initially. Run the orchestrated version in parallel with existing choreography for validation. Once confident, cut over and remove the old event-driven coordination. This gives you incremental wins and learning. Importantly: don't try to centralize everything. Some flows genuinely work well as choreography. The goal is right-sizing coordination approach to workflow complexity, not architectural purity.
