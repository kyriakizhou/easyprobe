### Human: I'm building out the investment thesis for a growth equity fund focused on climate tech. We're seeing massive capital flows into the space but valuations seem disconnected from fundamentals in many cases. How should we be thinking about sector selection and valuation discipline in this environment?

### Assistant: Climate tech growth equity is experiencing classic capital abundance dynamics - abundant capital chasing limited quality opportunities, compressing returns and elevating valuations. The challenge is distinguishing genuine technology-enabled differentiation from commoditized solutions dressed in climate language. Sector selection framework: prioritize areas where technology creates defensible competitive advantage rather than just enabling market access, where unit economics can achieve profitability without perpetual subsidy dependency, and where the market structure allows for value capture rather than commoditization. Within climate tech broadly, there's significant variation - deep tech with IP moats versus project deployment versus software/analytics versus consumer clean tech. Each has different risk-return profiles. What's your target check size and stage, and are you sector-agnostic within climate or focused on specific verticals?

### Human: $30-50M checks, Series B through pre-IPO. We're looking across sectors but finding the most interesting opportunities in industrial decarbonization and grid infrastructure. Solar and EVs feel overcrowded and commoditized.

### Assistant: Industrial decarbonization and grid infrastructure are strategically sound focuses - less crowded than consumer-facing climate tech, higher barriers to entry, and critical to actual decarbonization pathways. Within industrial decarbonization: differentiate between process innovation (new chemistry, new engineering approaches) which has higher technical risk but stronger moats, versus electrification plays which are more execution-dependent and potentially commoditizing. Grid infrastructure: software and analytics layers often have better return profiles than hardware, but hardware with genuine performance differentiation can be compelling. Valuation discipline in these sectors: revenue quality matters enormously - is revenue from commercial customers at scale or from pilot projects and grants? Is there technology lock-in or switching risk? Are margins reflective of competitive dynamics or subsidized customer acquisition? For your check size, what's your target ownership percentage and how are you thinking about reserve allocation for follow-ons?

### Human: Target 10-15% ownership, with 1:1 reserves for follow-on. We're finding that competitive rounds are pushing us to accept lower ownership or pay higher valuations than our models support.

### Assistant: Competitive round dynamics forcing ownership compression and valuation discipline erosion is the core tension in deployed capital environments. Options: accept lower ownership in highest-quality assets (thesis that great companies will outperform even at higher entry points), maintain valuation discipline and accept that you'll lose competitive deals (thesis that entry price matters and you'd rather miss deals than overpay), or differentiate on dimensions other than price to win deals at acceptable valuations (value-add, network, expertise, speed/certainty). The third option is where growth equity firms increasingly compete. In climate tech specifically: operational expertise in navigating regulatory complexity, customer introductions in industrial verticals, experience with project finance and infrastructure financing, and understanding of policy dynamics can be genuinely valuable and may justify lower valuations from founders. What's your team's distinctive capability that would make a founder prefer your capital at lower valuation than a competitor's at higher?

### Human: We have former operating executives from industrials and utilities on the team, which helps with customer access and operational credibility. But we're still losing deals to larger funds paying premium prices.

### Assistant: Larger funds paying premium prices may be making rational decisions within their own framework (need to deploy capital, longer hold periods, different return targets) but that doesn't mean you should match them. The strategic question: are you losing deals you should be winning, or deals that were never right for your fund? If larger funds are systematically outbidding you on the best opportunities, that's a problem. If they're overpaying for marginal opportunities that you're right to pass on, that's discipline. Tactical approaches: earlier relationship building - engage companies at Series A before competitive Series B dynamics; proprietary sourcing through your industrial network (can your operating executives identify portfolio companies before they hit the market?); structured transactions that address founder concerns other than price (governance, secondary liquidity, strategic support); and targeting slightly less competitive situations (perhaps companies outside traditional VC geographies, or sectors your team knows that generalist climate funds don't). Also: track the outcomes of deals you lost to higher bidders - if those companies consistently achieve the valuations, your model may be too conservative; if they struggle, your discipline is validated.

### Human: How should we be thinking about policy risk in climate tech? A lot of the value creation depends on regulatory support that could change.

### Assistant: Policy risk in climate tech is real but often poorly analyzed. Disaggregate it: direct subsidy dependency (ITC, PTC, 45Q tax credits) versus regulatory mandate (renewable portfolio standards, emissions limits) versus policy-influenced economics (carbon pricing, efficiency standards). Each has different risk profiles. Direct subsidies: most at risk from political shifts, but IRA credits have bipartisan constituency due to geographic distribution of benefits and long duration - risk is lower than headline politics suggests. Regulatory mandates: stickier than subsidies because they create compliance infrastructure, but can be weakened or delayed. Policy-influenced economics: often the most durable because they shift market structure. For your underwriting: stress-test each investment under policy scenarios - what happens if key subsidies phase out on schedule, accelerate, or are eliminated? Companies should have a pathway to subsidy-independent economics within your hold period, even if subsidies accelerate near-term returns. Also distinguish between policy risk for the company specifically versus the sector broadly - sector policy changes affect all competitors equally, company-specific policy exposure (concentrated in one jurisdiction, dependent on specific program) is higher risk.
