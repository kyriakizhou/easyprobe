### Assistant: The spread compression and covenant erosion you're describing reflects the competitive dynamics of too much capital chasing limited middle market deal flow. This creates a challenging risk management environment because traditional protections are being negotiated away precisely when you need them most. Several considerations: portfolio construction becomes more critical: when individual deal protections weaken, portfolio-level diversification and concentration limits matter more. If you can't get maintenance covenants, you need to be more conservative on position sizing and sector concentration. A covenant-lite deal to a cyclical business at 5x leverage is a different risk than to a recession-resistant business at 4x. Credit selection discipline: the temptation is to maintain deployment pace by accepting weaker terms. The funds that outperform through cycles are usually those willing to lose deals on terms rather than win deals that blow up. What's your current decline rate on deals you see? If it's not increasing as terms deteriorate, that's a warning sign. Structural protections that still matter: even in covenant-lite deals, focus on the protections you can still negotiate - EBITDA addback caps, restricted payment baskets, incremental debt capacity limits, change of control provisions. These may not prevent deterioration but they limit sponsor self-help at your expense. What's your current portfolio leverage and sector concentration looking like?

### Human: We've done stress testing but I'm not confident in our methodology - it feels like we're applying haircuts somewhat arbitrarily. What's a more rigorous framework for stress testing a middle market credit portfolio?

### Abstract: Most middle market stress testing is indeed arbitrary haircuts dressed up with spreadsheets. A more rigorous framework: bottom-up, deal-level stress analysis: for each position, model specific stress scenarios based on that business's actual risk factors. A healthcare staffing business has different stress drivers than a software company. Identify the 2-3 things that would actually impair each credit and model those specifically. Revenue stress should reflect the business model - recurring revenue businesses might see 10-15% stress, project-based businesses might see 30-40%. This is more work than applying uniform haircuts but produces meaningful output. EBITDA margin degradation: don't just stress revenue. In a recession, margins typically compress due to operating leverage and inability to reduce fixed costs quickly. Model margin compression of 200-500 bps depending on cost structure. For heavily labor-dependent businesses, assume labor costs are sticky while revenue falls. Cash flow, not just EBITDA: model the actual cash flow under stress - working capital swings, capex requirements, debt service coverage. A business might be EBITDA positive but cash flow negative in stress, especially with stretched payables or inventory builds. Liquidity runway: for each deal under stress, how many months until they breach covenants (if any) or run out of liquidity? This drives your workout timeline and optionality. Recovery analysis: model what recovery looks like if the deal does go to restructuring. What's the enterprise value in a sale, what's your position in the capital structure, what's the realistic recovery rate? Historical benchmarking: compare your assumptions to actual middle market default and recovery data. Moody's and others publish this. If your stress scenarios produce significantly better outcomes than historical experience, challenge your assumptions. Output should be: expected loss by deal under base and stress scenarios, portfolio-level loss estimate, liquidity needs for workout activity, concentration of stress in particular sectors or sponsors.
