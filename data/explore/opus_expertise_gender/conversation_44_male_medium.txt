### Human: I've been doing UX design for about 4 years at a mid-size company. I'm starting to feel like my skills are getting stale. How do I keep growing when I'm the only designer and there's no one senior to learn from?

### Assistant: Being a solo designer plateaus many people because you lack mentorship and become the default "expert" without external challenge to your assumptions. The skills that got you here may not be what you need for the next level. Common gaps for solo mid-level designers: strategic thinking beyond screens (business context, service design), advanced research methods, design systems thinking, and leadership/stakeholder management. What do you feel is most missing - technical skills, strategic skills, or exposure to different ways of working?

### Human: Probably a mix of strategic skills and exposure. I feel like I'm solving the same types of problems the same ways. I know there are better approaches but I don't see them in practice. I also don't have anyone pushing back on my decisions.

### Abstract: Both problems are solvable, but they require intentional effort outside your job. For exposure to different approaches: engage with design community online - designtwitter, UX communities, and forums like Designer News have working designers sharing process and decisions. Follow designers at companies you admire. Join design Slack communities (Mixed Methods, Hexagon, others) where practitioners discuss real problems. These become virtual colleagues. Portfolio reviews and critiques - ADPList offers free mentorship sessions with senior designers. Getting your work critiqued by someone with different experience reveals blind spots. Case study analysis - study how companies you respect approach design problems. Reverse-engineer their decisions. Figma Community has real files to examine. For strategic skill development: get closer to business metrics. Ask your stakeholders what success means for them, not just what they want designed. Understand how your work connects to business outcomes. Start tying your design decisions to business impact. Side projects with different constraints - redesigning an existing product for different audiences, or contributing to open source projects, forces you to approach problems differently. For external challenge: find a design mentor outside your company (ADPList, networking, conferences). Someone who can review your work periodically and ask questions you're not asking yourself. Consider joining a community of practice or starting a local design meetup.

### Human: The mentorship idea appeals to me. How do I find a good mentor when I don't have an existing network of senior designers?

### Abstract: Building mentor relationships without existing network is very achievable: ADPList (mentioned before) is purpose-built for this. Senior designers volunteer to mentor - you book free sessions. Start with several different mentors to find someone you click with, then ask for recurring sessions. It's explicitly designed for people in your situation. LinkedIn cold outreach: identify designers at your level+1 or +2 at companies you admire. Send thoughtful messages - not "will you be my mentor?" but "I'm a mid-level designer working on X type of problems. I really admired your case study on Y - could I ask you a few questions about your approach?" If that goes well, the relationship can develop. Many senior designers remember being where you are. Local design community: attend meetups, conferences, or workshops in your area. Connections made in person often develop into mentor relationships more naturally than cold outreach. Volunteer to help organize events - you meet people by working alongside them. Online communities: become a regular contributor in design Slack/Discord communities. Help others, share your work, ask thoughtful questions. Relationships develop over time. People you interact with regularly become informal mentors. Paid options: if budget allows, services like Intro.co connect you with senior practitioners for paid sessions. Also consider coaching programs specific to design leadership. The mentor relationship usually evolves from multiple interactions rather than one ask. Show up, be genuinely curious, give back where you can, and relationships develop.
