### Human: I'm leading the digital transformation of supply chain operations for a global consumer goods company. We're investing heavily in visibility and analytics capabilities, but I'm struggling to drive adoption across our regional teams. The technology is there but the organizational change isn't happening. What's missing?

### Assistant: Technology deployment without organizational adoption is the most common failure mode in supply chain digital transformation. The pattern: significant investment in platforms and tools, low utilization, reversion to legacy processes, and eventual write-off or reattempt. What's missing is usually one or more of: clear value articulation at the user level (what's in it for the regional planner versus the global objective), process redesign that embeds the new capabilities (if people can do their jobs the old way, they will), capability building that enables adoption (training, support, change management), and incentive alignment (metrics and rewards that drive desired behaviors). For global consumer goods specifically, regional autonomy is often strong - regions may view global initiatives as imposed overhead rather than enabling capability. What's the specific capability you're deploying and where are you seeing resistance?

### Human: We've deployed a demand sensing platform that integrates POS data, external signals, and ML forecasting. Global sees huge value in forecast accuracy improvement, but regional demand planners are still primarily using their spreadsheet-based processes and only engaging with the platform superficially.

### Assistant: Demand sensing adoption failure is classic - the platform provides better forecasts, but planners don't trust it or don't know how to use it in their workflows. Root causes to investigate: accuracy perception gap - do regional planners believe the ML forecasts are actually better than their judgment-based forecasts? Have you demonstrated performance comparison in their specific contexts? Trust requires proof. Process integration - where does the demand sensing output fit in their planning workflow? If it's an additional system to check rather than an integrated part of their process, it adds burden without reducing effort. Local knowledge incorporation - planners often have context (promotional plans, customer intelligence, local events) that the platform doesn't capture. If they feel the platform ignores their knowledge, they'll ignore it. Accountability structures - if planners are accountable for forecast accuracy and they don't trust the platform, using their own judgment is rational from their perspective - they'd rather be wrong their way than wrong the platform's way. What's the measured forecast accuracy comparison between platform and planner-generated forecasts in regions with low adoption?

### Human: Honestly, we haven't done rigorous comparison at the regional level. Global accuracy metrics show the platform is better, but we haven't disaggregated that to show each region how they compare.

### Assistant: Not demonstrating regional-level accuracy comparison is likely a significant factor. Planners don't experience "global accuracy improvement" - they experience their specific forecasts for their specific products and customers. If you can't show them that the platform outperforms their methods in their context, why would they trust it? Immediate action: run retrospective accuracy analysis at the regional and planner level. For the past 6-12 months, compare platform forecasts to planner forecasts to actual demand, at the granularity planners work (SKU-location or customer-SKU). Show each planner where the platform would have helped and where their judgment added value. This analysis likely reveals: platform significantly better in some contexts (stable demand, good data availability), planner judgment better in others (promotional periods, new products, data gaps), and some contexts where neither is great. Use this to build trust and inform workflow design. Change management approach: position the platform as augmenting planner expertise rather than replacing it. The platform handles the computational heavy lifting (baseline demand, signal processing); the planner adds judgment for factors the platform can't capture. This respects their expertise while creating a role for the technology. What's the current change management support structure - do you have dedicated resources working with regional teams on adoption?

### Human: We have a small central team that did initial training and provides support, but they're spread thin across 12 regions. Most engagement is reactive when regions have questions rather than proactive adoption support.

### Assistant: Small central team spread across 12 regions providing reactive support is insufficient for transformation-level change. The math: meaningful change management requires sustained engagement over months, not training sessions followed by help desk support. Proactive adoption support means embedding with regional teams, observing workflows, solving integration problems in real-time, and building relationships. You can't do that across 12 regions with a small central team. Options: regional change champions - identify and empower demand planning leaders in each region who become local adoption advocates. Provide them dedicated support, training, and incentives to drive adoption in their region. They have the relationships and local knowledge the central team lacks. Prioritized rollout - rather than spreading thin across all regions, concentrate change management resources on 2-3 regions, drive successful adoption with intensive support, document the playbook, then expand. Success stories and peer examples are powerful adoption drivers. Regional ownership - make regional leadership accountable for adoption metrics with appropriate support. If adoption is a global initiative imposed on regions with no regional ownership, it will be deprioritized against local priorities. Hybrid approach: put regional champions in place with clear accountability, support them with concentrated central team resources in waves, and use early success regions to build case studies and peer pressure for lagging regions. What's the regional leadership engagement - do regional supply chain leaders see platform adoption as their priority or as global's priority?

### Human: Regional leaders are evaluated on traditional metrics - service levels, inventory turns, cost. Platform adoption isn't explicitly in their objectives. They're supportive in principle but it's not their priority.

### Assistant: If platform adoption isn't in regional leaders' objectives but traditional metrics are, rational regional leaders will focus on traditional metrics. Platform adoption becomes something they're "supportive of in principle" but won't invest real attention in - exactly what you're experiencing. This is a governance and incentive problem, not a technology or change management problem. The solution: make platform adoption a measured objective for regional leadership, tied to their performance evaluation and compensation. Options for how to frame the adoption metric: leading indicators - platform engagement metrics (active users, forecast acceptance rates, system utilization) as explicit objectives; lagging indicators - forecast accuracy improvement, with expectation that platform utilization is the means; hybrid - adoption metrics in year one transitioning to performance metrics in year two as capability matures. The conversation with senior leadership: "We've invested $X in this platform. Adoption is failing because regional leaders have no incentive to prioritize it. We need to either add adoption to their objectives or accept that the investment won't deliver expected returns." This escalates the decision appropriately - it's not a change management team problem to solve, it's a leadership alignment problem. If leadership isn't willing to make adoption an explicit objective, that tells you something about actual organizational commitment to the transformation. What's your relationship with the executive sponsor who can make this incentive alignment happen?
