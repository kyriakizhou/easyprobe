### Human: I'm the chief architect at a large insurance company. We're undertaking a core system modernization - replacing our 30-year-old policy administration system. This is a multi-year, bet-the-company initiative. What are the critical success factors we should be focused on?

### Assistant: Core policy administration system replacement is among the highest-risk technology initiatives in insurance. The failure rate is sobering - many of these programs significantly overrun on time and cost, and some are abandoned entirely. The stakes are existential: the system processes your core product, and failure can disrupt business for years. Critical success factors: executive sponsorship and patience: this is not an IT project, it's a business transformation. You need executive sponsorship that understands the multi-year timeline and protects the program from budget pressures, organizational politics, and scope thrash. Sponsors must have the authority to make difficult decisions (which products to migrate first, when to pause for stabilization) and the patience to see it through leadership changes. Scope and sequencing discipline: the temptation is to modernize everything simultaneously - new system, process improvements, product simplification, data cleanup. This multiplies risk. Successful programs sequence carefully: get the new system working for a manageable scope, prove it works, then expand. The "big bang" approach fails more often than incremental migration. Vendor and implementation partner selection: if you're using a commercial platform (Guidewire, Duck Creek, Majesco, etc.), vendor selection is foundational. Equally important is implementation partner capability. Look for partners with deep insurance domain expertise and specific experience with your chosen platform. Check references extensively - not just that they implemented the platform, but how the implementation went, what the actual timeline was, and whether the client would do it again. Data migration reality: 30 years of policy data carries 30 years of data quality issues, schema changes, and undocumented business logic encoded in data. Data migration is often 40-50% of total effort and the primary source of timeline risk. What's driving the initiative - regulatory pressure, cost, competitive need, or system end-of-life?

### Human: Primary driver is that our current vendor is sunsetting support for our platform version, and upgrading within the current platform is almost as expensive as moving to a modern system. We've selected a platform and an implementation partner, but we're now planning the migration approach. We're debating between big-bang conversion versus gradual migration by product line.

### Abstract: Given your situation - forced migration, platform selected, now planning approach - the big-bang versus gradual decision is indeed critical and should be made with eyes open about the tradeoffs. Big-bang conversion (all products/policies at once): advantages: single cutover, no need to run parallel systems long-term, cleaner end state, avoids integration complexity between old and new systems. Risks: extremely high stakes cutover - if something goes wrong, your entire book is affected. Requires extensive testing with limited ability to validate at production scale before cutover. Rollback is often impractical. No real production learning before the bulk of the migration. When it works: smaller books of business, simpler product portfolios, organizations with high testing discipline, or when running parallel systems is technically impractical. Gradual migration by product line: advantages: production learning on lower-volume products before high-volume migration. Risk contained to migrated segment. Rollback is possible (keep policies on old system). Teams learn and improve between phases. Issues discovered early affect fewer policies. Risks: longer total timeline. Requires integration layer between old and new systems during transition. Operational complexity of running two systems (training, support, reporting). "Migration fatigue" - organization may lose momentum before completing. When it works: larger, complex books with distinct product lines. Organizations that can sustain multi-year programs. When product lines have natural separation. For a 30-year-old system with likely significant data complexity, gradual migration is typically lower risk despite longer timeline. The learning from early product migrations reduces risk for subsequent phases. The key question: do you have product lines that are naturally separable and could serve as initial migration candidates (ideally lower volume, less complex)?

### Human: We do have some natural product line separation - personal lines and commercial lines are fairly distinct, and within commercial we have some specialty products that are self-contained. How should we think about sequencing which products to migrate first?

### Abstract: Sequencing strategy should balance risk reduction (learn before tackling hardest problems) with business value (demonstrate progress on meaningful scope). The classic mistake is starting with either the easiest products (doesn't prove the hard stuff works) or the highest-volume products (maximum risk for first attempt). Sequencing framework: first migration - de-risking scope: choose a product line that is: self-contained with minimal dependencies on other products, modest volume (enough to validate at scale, not so much that problems are catastrophic), representative enough of core capabilities to validate the platform and approach, owned by a business team willing to partner closely and tolerate some disruption. This first migration is as much about organizational learning as about migrating business. You're proving the approach, building muscle memory, discovering integration gaps, and establishing patterns for subsequent migrations. Expect to invest more heavily in this phase than pure cost-per-policy would suggest. Second migration - capability expansion: expand to a product with additional complexity - different underwriting rules, different billing patterns, different regulatory requirements. This validates platform flexibility and surfaces issues not present in the first product. Still not your highest-volume book. Subsequent migrations - accelerating with confidence: once patterns are proven and teams are experienced, migrate higher-volume and higher-complexity books. The later phases should move faster than early phases because the organization has learned. Specific recommendation based on your structure: consider starting with a specialty commercial product that's self-contained. Personal lines and core commercial are likely too high-volume for first migration. A specialty product proves commercial capability while limiting blast radius. After that, either tackle another specialty or prove personal lines capability (depending on platform and business priority) before the core commercial migration.
